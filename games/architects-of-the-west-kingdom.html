Learn it properly · 14 points

Architects of theWest Kingdom

Twenty workers, one placed per turn, and no round ever ends to give them back. The person who takes them off the board is sitting opposite you, and they are doing you a favour by accident.

1 The big picture

A worker placement game where nothing is blocked and nothing comes back on its own.

You are a royal architect in the Carolingian west, around 850 AD, competing to build landmarks for a king who is not paying much attention. Points come from buildings you construct and levels of the cathedral you fund, with a trickle from gold, marble and silver at the end.

Two things make it unlike the worker placement game you have played before. First, locations are not exclusive. Everyone can pile onto the forest at once, and the more of your own workers stand there, the more wood you take. Nobody can block you out of a space.

Second, and this is the whole game: there are no rounds. No phase ever comes along to hand your workers back. You have twenty of them, you place one per turn, and once one is standing in the forest it stands there until somebody physically removes it. Usually that somebody is an opponent, hiring thugs at the Town Centre to march your crew off the board.

Running alongside all of it is a virtue track. Shortcuts are freely available: rob the tax pot, deal on the black market, throw people in prison for pocket money. None of it is punished when you do it. All of it is judged in one lump at the end.

You are not fighting for spaces, because there are always spaces. You are fighting over labour, over the small pot of tax silver, and over how many turns are left before the Guildhall fills up and the game stops.

Remember this

Workers are not a renewable resource on a timer. They are capital you invest and then have to go and recover. Every other rule in the game is downstream of that.

2 Your turn

Place one worker. That is the entire turn.

On your turn you take one worker from your player board, put it on a location, and resolve that location. Then it is the next player's turn. There is no second action, no bonus phase, no card play step. A four player game is four people each doing that, over and over, for about a hundred turns between them.

Because the turn is so small, the game is fast at the table and slow in your head. The only decision you ever make is where, and the reason that is hard is that every placement is also a commitment: that worker is out of your hand for a long time, possibly forever.

The one exception

If you start your turn with no workers left on your board, your entire turn is spent walking one worker home from the main board, and you do not get to place it until next turn. You cannot fetch one from the Guildhall, the Black Market, the Prison, or from an opponent's board, so it has to come from a location you actually wanted it standing in.

That turn is the worst turn in the game. You spend a full turn to undo a previous turn and gain nothing. New players walk into it around the middle of the game, having placed nineteen workers without ever thinking about how any of them get back.

Remember this

Running out of workers is not a soft limit, it is a penalty turn. Plan your recovery before you are down to your last two.

3 Worker investment

The second worker at a location is worth more than the first one was.

The four resource locations pay you by how many of your own workers are standing there once you have placed, opponents' workers being irrelevant. Your first worker in the forest gets 1 wood. Your second gets 2 wood. Your third gets 3 wood. You are not paid for the trip, you are paid for the size of the crew.

That single rule creates the tension the whole game runs on. Spreading one worker across four locations is four turns for four items. Stacking four workers into one location is four turns for ten items. Concentration is dramatically better, and concentration is exactly what makes a fat, tempting target for the neighbour with a purse of silver.

4 Capture

The main way workers come home is that somebody takes them.

The Town Centre is where you hire locals to seize workers. For each of your own workers standing at the Town Centre, you may spend 1 silver to capture one whole colour group from one location: not one worker, the entire stack of that colour standing there. Captured workers of another colour go onto the top of your player board, where they sit as hostages.

Now the part that reframes the game. You may capture your own workers, and when you do they go straight back into your supply, ready to place again. That is not a spiteful move, it is the standard recall action. You spend one turn and a coin, and four workers walk home in a single trip.

So the loop is: invest workers into a stack, harvest the growing returns for as long as you can stand it, then spend one turn at the Town Centre to bring the whole stack back. The stack is both your income and your labour pool.

With two or three players you may capture from up to two locations in a turn; with four or more, only one. The first silver you spend on capturing is tax, which does not go back to the bank but onto the tax pot, and point 7 explains why that matters.

The trap

New players read capture as an attack and feel rude doing it. It is mostly logistics. If you never visit the Town Centre you will spend the second half of the game taking penalty turns walking workers home one at a time.

Remember this

Capturing your own stack is the efficient way to reload. Capturing someone else's is a bonus you take on the way past.

5 Prison and the Guardhouse

Hostages are worth a coin each, and the person you took them from has to spend a turn.

Captured workers sitting on your board are not points. To cash them in you go to the Guardhouse and send them to prison, gaining 1 silver each. It is small money, but it is money you got from someone else's turns.

The Guardhouse gives you one action for each of your own workers there, from this list:

  • Jail your hostages: send all captured workers on your board to the prison, 1 silver each.
  • Bail yourself out: take all of your own workers out of the prison, free, back onto your board.
  • Ransom: pay 5 silver, or take a debt and lose 1 virtue, to reclaim all your workers sitting on opponents' boards.
  • Clear a debt: pay 6 silver to flip one debt card over, which also gains you 1 virtue.

So captivity is annoying rather than fatal: your workers come back, but only when you spend a turn on it. The genuine cost is the prison itself, which bites twice. Every two of your workers sitting in prison at the end is minus 1 VP, and having three or more of them in there when a Black Market Reset happens costs you virtue, or a debt if you are the worst offender.

Remember this

Being captured costs you a turn. Being left in prison costs you points. Clear the cells before the reckoning, not after.

6 Virtue

A dial from 0 to 14 that is not a morality meter. It is a choice of lane.

Everyone starts at 7, the exact middle. Dozens of actions nudge you up or down: hire a certain apprentice, build a certain building, rob the tax stand, take a black market deal, pay off a debt. Nothing happens at the moment you move. The track only cashes out at the end, and it cashes out hard, from a fat bonus at the top to a punishing loss at the bottom.

The clever part is that the track is also a set of gates, and they point in opposite directions:

Down at 3 or less you stop paying tax, which makes every apprentice and every capture cheaper for the rest of the game. Up at 10 or more the black market shuts its door to you, but the cathedral and the endgame flags are yours. Sitting at 7 gives you neither the discount nor the bonus.

The trap

Drifting. Most first games end with two players at 6 or 8, having scored roughly zero from the track while paying full price for everything all game. Pick a direction in the first few turns and lean on it.

Remember this

Virtue is a commitment, not a conscience. Low virtue is a live discount all game; high virtue is a lump sum at the end. Half measures buy neither.

7 Tax

The honest players fill a pot, and anyone is allowed to walk off with it.

Some costs are marked in red coins. Those are tax, and they do not go back to the bank, they go into a space above the Tax Stand. Hiring an apprentice costs 4 silver of which 2 are tax. Your first capture each turn is taxed. Paying off a debt is 6 silver of which 3 are tax.

The Tax Stand is a location like any other. Put a worker there and you take every coin in the pot and lose 2 virtue. Nothing else. Early it might be 5 silver, and by the middle of a busy game it can be a dozen or more, which is an enormous swing for one turn.

What that feels like

Three players have been hiring apprentices for six turns. The pot is at 11. You are already down at virtue 4, so you were not going to score from the top of the track anyway.

One worker, 11 silver, virtue 4 to 2. And now you also stop paying tax entirely.

This is why the low road is a real strategy rather than a stumble. Falling down the track pays you twice: once in the pot, and once in every tax you never pay again. The cost is a bad number on the flag at the end, so the low player has to convert that silver into buildings fast enough to outrun it.

Remember this

The pot is a shared clock nobody is watching. Look at it every turn, and know who is low enough on virtue not to care about taking it.

8 The Black Market

Three chairs, cheap goods, and a raid that empties them into the cells.

The Black Market has exactly three single spaces, unlike everywhere else on the board. One worker per space, first come first served. Each buys a bundle at a discount: silver plus 1 virtue for a fistful of marble, gold and stone, or the pick of any faceup apprentice, or a dig through five building plans to keep the best one.

These are genuinely good deals, and they are the fastest route to marble in the game. They cost virtue every time, and you are locked out entirely once you sit at 10 or more.

The reset

When the third space fills, or when a worker lands on one of the trigger spaces in the Guildhall, there is a Black Market Reset. Every worker in the market goes to the prison, and the market refreshes with a new, larger set of offers. Then the accounting: anyone with three or more workers in prison loses a virtue, and whoever has the most in there takes a debt.

So a black market worker is not stranded, it is pre-committed to prison, and the only question is when. If your cells are already crowded, taking the third chair yourself hands you the fine you were trying to avoid.

Remember this

Every black market deal is a worker you have already sent to jail. Take the deal when the prison is empty, and clear the cells before someone fills the third chair.

9 The King's Storehouse

The honest conversion desk: bulk goods into marble, or into virtue.

The Storehouse pays you one action per worker you have there, and you may repeat the same one. The two printed options are to trade 2 of the common goods for 1 virtue, or 3 wood and stone for 1 marble.

That is the counterweight to the black market. Wood and stone are the things you gather fastest and in the largest heaps, and on their own they only buy the cheaper buildings. The Storehouse turns that surplus into the two things that are otherwise scarce: marble, which is 1 VP each and the price of the fancier buildings, and virtue, which you can buy in bulk with goods rather than begging for it from cards.

It is also where several apprentices plug in, adding extra trades to the list, so a Storehouse stack with the right hires becomes a small factory rather than a single conversion.

Remember this

Virtue is purchasable. If you want the top of the track, you do not need to be nice, you need a pile of clay and a few workers standing in the Storehouse.

10 Apprentices

They are not bonuses. They are permission slips.

The Workshop does two jobs. The first is hiring apprentices, at 4 silver each, of which 2 is tax. The catch is reach: your first worker at the Workshop can only hire from the leftmost column, your second from the first two, and so on, so a deep Workshop stack sees the whole row. In a hurry you can pay 1 silver to shove aside a card you do not want, and whoever hires that card later pockets the coin.

You may have at most five apprentices at a time, though you can discard one to make room. Some abilities are ongoing, some fire on a Black Market Reset, some add trades at the Storehouse. Several move your virtue the moment you hire them, and discarding does not undo that.

The part first time players miss: apprentices carry the three craft skills, carpentry, tiling and masonry, and many of the good buildings simply cannot be constructed unless you have hired an apprentice with the matching skill. The apprentice is not spent when you build, it just has to be there.

The trap

Holding a beautiful 12 point building card all game and discovering at the Guildhall that you never hired a mason. Read your building plans first, then go shopping for the skills they demand.

The Workshop's other action is drawing new building plans: 1 card, plus 1 more for every 2 workers you have there, up to a hand limit of 6.

Remember this

Apprentices are the gate on which half of the deck you are allowed to build. Hire early, while the cheap turns are still cheap.

11 Buildings

Where most of your points come from, paid for in the goods you have been hoarding.

You draft 3 building plans before the first turn and collect more at the Workshop and the black market. A plan is just a card in hand until you take it to the Guildhall, pay its resource cost, satisfy any skill icons, and lay it faceup in front of you.

Buildings score straightforwardly and heavily. The cheap ones are a couple of wood and stone for a few points; the expensive ones want gold, marble and all three skills, and pay double figures with a bonus attached, such as points for the workers you jailed. Many also swing your virtue on construction, up for a chapel, sharply down for a keep or a gate, which is another reason your lane on the track has to be chosen and not stumbled into.

Two ends of the deck

A well needs no skills at all: 2 wood, 2 stone, worth 3 VP, and it hands you 4 clay the moment it lands.

A keep needs carpentry, tiling and masonry, plus 5 wood, 2 stone and 2 gold, drops you 2 virtue, and is worth 12 VP with more on top for prisoners.

Same one turn at the Guildhall. Everything before it is the difference.

Remember this

Resources are not points, buildings are. A tidy pile of goods at the end is a pile of turns you wasted.

12 The Cathedral

A public ladder where only one architect can stand on each rung.

Instead of building for yourself, a Guildhall worker can advance the cathedral. You pay the cost of the level directly above your marker, discard a building card from your hand, move up one level, and turn over a reward card for an immediate gift. At the end you score the number printed on the level you reached, and the higher levels are worth serious points.

Three things make it more interesting than a points track. First, the cost is in bulk goods, mostly wood and stone in any mix, so it is the natural sink for the material a big forest stack produces. Second, each level holds one player, so if someone is sitting on the rung above you, you are stuck until they climb. Third, you are barred from cathedral work entirely at 4 virtue or less.

That last rule is what makes the two lanes genuinely different games. The saintly player builds the cathedral and takes the flag at the top of the virtue track. The crooked player cannot touch it and has to win on buildings, stolen tax and cheap black market goods instead.

Remember this

The cathedral is a queue, not a track. Waiting for a better turn can mean the rung is gone and the door has closed behind your own falling virtue.

13 The Guildhall

Every point you score also burns the clock, and the clock is the Guildhall.

There is no round track and no scoring rounds. The game ends when the last worker space in the Guildhall is filled, after which everyone including that player takes one final turn.

Read that against the previous two points. Constructing a building takes a Guildhall space. Advancing the cathedral takes a Guildhall space. Those are the only two ways to score real points, and they are the only two things that shorten the game. Workers laid down there are laid flat and never come back: they cannot be captured, cannot be recalled, and are gone from your twenty for good.

Which means the length of the game is set by whoever is building. If three players are laying down cheap buildings every few turns, the Guildhall fills quickly and your slow, beautiful engine never gets to run. Count the empty spaces the way you would count a deck running out.

Remember this

Nobody announces the last round. The Guildhall announces it, several turns in advance, in plain sight.

14 Your first game

How not to lose it.

Everything up to here is the game. This is the part where the habits from other games betray you.

  • Do not spread your workers one per location. That instinct is correct in a game with blocking, and here it converts your twenty workers into twenty single items.
  • Go to the Town Centre and capture your own crew. It is not aggression, it is the reload button, and there is no other cheap one.
  • Pick a lane on the virtue track in the first five turns. Ending at 7 is scoring nothing from a track that swings by well over ten points.
  • Watch the tax pot. If it is fat and someone at the table is sitting low on virtue, it is already theirs. If it is fat and you are low on virtue, take it.
  • Empty your prison cells. Two workers in there is a point, and three of them is a virtue loss at the next reset.
  • Read your building plans before you hire. Skills gate construction, and an apprentice you needed can be two visits away when the Guildhall is nearly full.
  • Spend your goods. Gold and marble are 1 VP each, but clay, wood and stone are worth precisely nothing in your supply at the end.
  • Count the Guildhall. The moment there are only a few spaces left, stop developing and start converting everything you own into cards on the table.

The whole game in one line

Twenty workers, one turn each, and the only questions that matter are how big your stacks get before you recall them and which end of the virtue track you meant to be standing at when the Guildhall fills.
