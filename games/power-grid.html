Learn it properly · 14 points · Friedemann Friese

Buy the plants, buy the fuel, wire the cities, and never be firstPower Grid

You run an electricity company. You bid for power stations, buy coal and oil and uranium off a market everyone shares, and string cables across a map that gets more crowded every round. It is an economic game about efficiency, and about the very odd fact that being in the lead is a handicap.

1 The big picture

The winner is whoever powers the most cities on the last turn.

Power Grid is for two to six players and runs about two hours. Each of you starts with a pile of money, called Elektro, and nothing else. Over the game you buy power plants at auction, buy fuel to run them, and build into cities on a map of a real country, connecting them into your own network.

Every round, each city you can power earns you money, which pays for more plants, more fuel and more cities. When somebody's network reaches a set size, the game ends, and the winner is the player who can power the most cities in that final round. Not the richest, not the biggest network: the most cities actually lit.

So money is never the goal. It is the engine. Points are cities, and the only cities that count are the ones you have the plants and the fuel to light at the very end.

Remember this

Three things have to line up: cities built, plant capacity, and fuel. The winner is limited by whichever of the three they have least of.

2 The round

Five phases, the same every round, and the order is the strategy.

Each round runs through the same sequence:

  • Turn order is set: whoever has the most cities goes first.
  • Auction: power plants are bid for, in turn order.
  • Buy fuel, in reverse turn order.
  • Build into cities, in reverse turn order.
  • Bureaucracy: fire your plants, power cities, collect money, and the markets restock.

Look at that list again. The player in the lead acts first in the auction, where going first is a disadvantage, and last for fuel and building, where going last is a disadvantage. That is not an accident. It is the central balancing idea of the whole game, and the next two points are about it.

Remember this

Turn order is recalculated every round from the map. Leading the city count is a cost you pay in every phase that follows.

3 The leader's tax

First in the auction, last to the shops. Being ahead costs you.

Why is going first bad in the auction? Because the first player has to open the bidding without knowing what anyone else wants, and everyone behind can wait and see which plants are left and how much money was spent.

Why is going last bad for fuel and building? Because the fuel market gets more expensive as it is bought (point 8), so the last player pays the highest prices. And the map gets more crowded with every city built (point 10), so the last builder finds the cheap spots taken.

Remember this

The game taxes the leader in every phase. Leading early is usually paying that tax for nothing.

4 Hang back

A slow start is not a mistake. It is often the plan.

Put point 1 and point 3 together and the strategy appears. Only the last round decides the winner, and every round before that, the player with the most cities is paying a tax. So good players often deliberately stay one or two cities behind for most of the game, saving money, buying cheaply, picking their building spots first, and then sprinting at the end.

This is the single most surprising thing about Power Grid for new players, who naturally build as much as they can afford every round. That player usually leads for an hour, pays top price for fuel the whole time, and loses to someone who looked a little behind until the final two rounds.

The trick is that hanging back must be deliberate. You still need the money and the plants to make the sprint; you just choose not to spend it on cities until the cities matter.

The instinct to unlearn

Most games reward being ahead. Here the scoreboard is the map, and the map punishes whoever is in front. Build when it earns you money or blocks a rival, not just because you can.

Remember this

Second place is a fine place to be all game. Just be ready to be first on the last turn.

5 Power plants

A plant is three numbers: its price, its fuel, and how many cities it lights.

Every power plant card shows a number, which is its minimum price and also a rough measure of how advanced it is. Then it shows what it burns and how much (say, two coal) and how many cities it can power when it runs (say, three).

Higher-numbered plants are more efficient: more cities for the same fuel, or even no fuel at all, in the case of wind and solar plants. Lower plants are cheap now and inefficient forever.

You can own only three plants at a time (four in a two-player game). Buying a fourth means scrapping one. So your plant line-up is a tiny portfolio, and every upgrade is also a replacement.

Remember this

Judge a plant by cities per fuel and cities per slot. With only three slots, a cheap plant is an expensive use of space.

6 The plant market

Eight plants on display, and you can only buy from the cheaper half.

Plants are laid out in two rows of four, sorted by number. The lower four are the current market: these are the only ones you can bid for. The upper four are the future market: visible, waiting, sliding down into the current row as cheaper ones are bought.

That gives the market a visible future. You can see the plant you want sitting in the upper row, and work out how many purchases have to happen before it drops down to where someone can buy it. You can also see when nobody will buy, because every plant on offer is bad, and the market stalls.

Plants enter from a deck that runs roughly from small to large across the game, so the plants on offer get better as the game goes on. Small plants that nobody wants are cleared out of the market over time, too.

Remember this

Read the future row every round. Buying a plant changes which plant drops into reach for the next bidder.

7 The auction

One plant each per round at most, and the money spent here is money not spent on cities.

In turn order, each player may put up one plant from the current market for auction, opening at its printed number at least. Everyone still interested bids up in turn; drop out and you are out of that auction. The winner pays and takes the plant. You can win at most one plant per round, so once you have bought, you sit out the rest of the auctions.

That last rule creates the quiet tactics of the phase. A player who has already bought is out; the players still waiting have fewer rivals for each plant. A player who passes on starting an auction might get a plant cheaper later, or might get nothing.

And every Elektro you spend over a plant's printed price is money not going into fuel or cities. Winning a bidding war for a plant you wanted by three Elektro might be fine. Winning it by twenty can cost you two cities.

What it looks like at the table

You want the 20 and so does the player who opened it. The bidding reaches 30. You stop and look at the future row: a 21 that does nearly the same job is next to drop in.

You drop out. Your rival pays 30, sits out the rest of the phase, and the 21 slides into the current row for you to open at its minimum.

Remember this

Know your top price before the bidding starts. A plant is worth what it earns you, not what someone else is willing to pay for it.

8 The fuel market

Every unit bought makes the next one more expensive.

Coal, oil, garbage and uranium sit on a shared market track, with the cheapest spaces filled last and emptied first. Buy two coal and you take the two cheapest; the next player's coal costs more. The market refills a little each round, but only a set amount, so heavy demand drives prices up and keeps them there.

This is the most direct interaction in the game. If three players all run coal plants, coal becomes painfully expensive, and the player who buys last (the leader, remember) pays the most. If you are the only oil player at the table, oil stays cheap and plentiful for you.

A plant can also store up to twice its fuel need. Buying extra is a way to secure next round's supply, and a way to make a fuel scarce for a rival who depends on it.

Remember this

Look at which fuels the other players burn before you buy a plant. The cheapest fuel is the one nobody else needs.

9 Five kinds of power

Coal, oil, garbage, uranium, and the wind that costs nothing to run.

Each fuel has its own personality, and the shape of the fuel market follows from how many cubes come back each round and who is competing for them.

  • Coal is plentiful and cheap early, and the fuel most players start on. That popularity is its problem.
  • Oil is a little scarcer. Some plants burn either coal or oil, which gives you a choice each round and protects you from a price spike in one of them.
  • Garbage arrives later and in smaller amounts. Few plants use it early, so it can be a cheap niche.
  • Uranium is rare and expensive, but uranium plants are efficient, and few rivals are competing with you for it.
  • Ecological plants (wind and solar) need no fuel at all. Pricey to win at auction, but free to run forever and immune to every market squeeze.
Remember this

Diversify away from your neighbours' fuel. A plant that burns what nobody else burns is cheaper every single round.

10 Building the network

Cities cost a fee to enter and a toll to reach, and the map fills up.

To build, you pay two things: the connection cost along the lines on the map from your existing network to the new city, and a fee for the slot in the city itself. The first company into a city pays the cheapest slot; the second, when allowed, pays more; the third pays more again.

Early on, each city has room for only one company. That makes the opening a land grab. If a rival builds into the cities around you first, the only way forward for you is a long, expensive connection through someone else's territory, or into a far corner of the map.

Geography matters a great deal. Some regions have clusters of cities joined by cheap links; others are sparse with expensive connections. A compact network in a cheap region can outbuild a sprawling one twice its size, simply because each new city costs less.

Remember this

Build where the connections are cheap, and do not let yourself be walled in. Early cities are as much about claiming space as about income.

11 The three steps

The game changes gear twice, and the map opens up each time.

Power Grid runs in three steps. In step 1, a city holds only one company. Once somebody's network reaches a certain size, step 2 begins, and every city opens a second slot. Later, when a special card turns up in the plant deck, step 3 begins, and a third slot opens.

Each step also changes the economy around it: the markets restock differently, the smallest plants get cleared away, and the plant market itself shifts. The effect is that the game feels like three different games stacked on top of each other: a cramped land grab, a long middle of steady growth, and an open final sprint.

Step 2 is worth watching. A player who triggers it opens second slots in every city, including the ones they had all to themselves. If you have walled off a region, you may not want to be the player who unlocks it.

Remember this

Each step unlocks more room on the map. Plan for the space that will open up, not just the space you have now.

12 Income

Each extra city powered pays a little less than the last.

In bureaucracy, you count the cities you can power and collect money from a table. Even powering nothing pays a little. Powering one city pays a good deal more. But the table flattens as it goes: going from four cities to five adds less than going from one to two, and powering fifteen cities earns only somewhat more than powering ten.

Two things follow. First, the income gap between leader and follower is smaller than the city gap, so hanging back (point 4) costs less money than it looks. Second, a city you build mid-game only pays for itself slowly. Late in the game, many cities are worth building only because of what they score at the end, not what they earn.

And you only collect for cities you actually power. A big network with too few plants or too little fuel earns from the part that runs.

Remember this

Income flattens as you grow. Extra cities in the middle game are bought for position, not for the money they bring in.

13 The finish

Somebody reaches the target, and the last round is a count of lights.

When any player's network reaches the end-game size (seventeen cities in a three- or four-player game, different at other counts), the game ends after that round. Then everyone powers what they can, one last time, and the most cities powered wins. Money only breaks ties.

That last round is where the sprint from point 4 pays off. The player who saved money can buy a big plant, fill it with fuel and build five or six cities in one go, while the early leader may have the cities but not quite the capacity or the cash to light them all.

Think backwards from it. In the final round you need three things to agree: enough cities, enough plant capacity, enough fuel. Whichever is smallest is your score. Spending the last of your money on a city you cannot power gains you nothing.

What it looks like at the table

It is clearly the final round: a rival will reach seventeen this turn. You have fourteen cities, plants that can power fifteen, and money for three more cities.

Build one city, not three. Fifteen powered is your ceiling this round, and the money from the other two might buy the fuel that keeps all fifteen lit.

Remember this

The final score is the smallest of cities, capacity and fuel. Balance all three on the last turn; surplus in any one of them is wasted.

14 How not to lose your first game

Five instincts other games gave you, and what they cost here.

You raced to the lead

Leading the city count puts you first in the auction and last to buy fuel and build. Do it in the last round, not the first five.

You won every bidding war

Every Elektro over a plant's printed price is money not spent on cities and fuel. Let rivals overpay; the next plant is often almost as good.

You burned the same fuel as everyone else

Shared fuel gets expensive, and the leader buys last. A plant on an unpopular fuel or no fuel at all saves you money every round.

You built wherever was nearest

Cheap connections matter, and so does space. A cramped corner surrounded by rivals means expensive building for the rest of the game.

You ended with cities you could not power

The winner is decided by cities powered, not owned. In the last round, match cities, plant capacity and fuel; a surplus in one is wasted.

The whole game in one line

Plants, fuel and cities, bought in an order that taxes the leader. Stay just behind, burn what nobody else burns, and make sure that on the last turn your cities, capacity and fuel all line up.
