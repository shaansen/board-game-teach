Learn it properly · 14 points

Six rounds, one map, fourteen peoples who all want the same groundTerra Mystica

Nobody ever attacks you in Terra Mystica. There are no dice and no cards in hand, and everything on the table is face up from the first minute. It is still one of the most ruthless games you will play, because the whole thing is a fight over a fixed amount of land, and every hex you want is a hex somebody else can take first.

1 The big picture

You are a people who can only live on one kind of ground, and the ground is wrong.

Two to five players each take one of fourteen fantasy peoples. Each people lives on exactly one of seven terrain types: the Witches live on forest, the Dwarves on mountain, the Nomads on desert, and so on. Your buildings can only ever stand on your own colour of terrain.

The map is a single shared board of about eighty hexes in mixed colours, and it does not change between games. So the game starts with a problem rather than an opportunity: the land you want is scattered, and most of the hexes near you are the wrong type. Making them right is called terraforming, and it is the main thing you spend your life doing.

You play six rounds. In each one you take income, then take actions one at a time until every player has passed. At the end you add up victory points, and the numbers are small: a decent first game is around 90 points, and the gap between winning and third place is often under ten. That matters more than it sounds. In a game where points are that tight, no single clever move wins it. Fifteen slightly cheaper moves do.

There is no combat, no theft and no card you can play on another player's turn. Interaction comes from two places only: the land runs out, and your neighbours hand you power every time they build. Both of those are covered below, and both are the game.

Remember this

Terra Mystica is not a game of big plays. It is a game about doing ordinary things for slightly less than everyone else pays.

2 The terrain wheel

Every hex on the map has a price, and the price is written on a wheel.

The seven terrain types sit in a fixed circle: plains, swamp, lake, forest, mountain, wasteland, desert, and then back round to plains. To change a hex from one type to another you move around that circle one step at a time, and each step costs one spade.

So if you are the Witches, whose colour is forest, a lake hex is one step away and costs one spade. A desert hex is three steps away and costs three. The same hex has a different price for every player at the table, and you can read that price off the wheel before anyone tells you.

A spade is not a thing you own, it is an amount of work. At the start it costs you three workers to produce one spade. Your player board has a terraforming track you can upgrade twice, dropping that to two workers, then one. Going from three workers per spade to one is the difference between a hex costing nine workers and costing three.

This is also why the wheel is the seating plan for the whole game. The factions sitting one or two steps from you on the wheel want hexes that are cheap for you, and you want hexes that are cheap for them. Your real opponent is not the loudest player, it is whoever shares your corner of the wheel.

Remember this

Before your first turn, look at the map and count steps. The hexes two spades away are the ones you will quietly never build on.

3 One action at a time

There is no big turn. There is a long sequence of small ones.

A round is not "everyone takes a turn". Play passes round the table and each player takes exactly one action, then the next player does, and so on, for as long as people keep going. Build a dwelling. That is your turn. Upgrade a building. That is your turn.

You keep going round until you decide to pass, which takes you out of the round entirely. Everyone else carries on without you. When the last player passes, the round ends and a new one starts with income.

Two consequences fall straight out of this, and they are both larger than they look.

  • Resources are pace. A player with more workers and coins simply gets more turns this round than you do. Running out of things to spend is how you pass early, and passing early is how you watch three hexes you wanted get taken.
  • The order of passing sets the next round. The first player to pass goes first next round. So passing early is not purely a loss: it buys you the first pick of the map next time, which in a tight game is sometimes exactly what you want.

New players tend to think of the round as a budget to spend down. It is better to think of it as an auction where the currency is workers and the item is position. Every action you take is one the player after you now has to respond to.

Remember this

Whoever can still afford actions after everyone else has passed is the player shaping the map. Income is turns.

4 The buildings are your income sheet

You are not building a village. You are uncovering a printed track.

You have five kinds of structure, and they live as physical pieces on your player board covering up numbers. Every time you build one, you take it off the board and reveal what was underneath, which is income you collect every round from then on.

StructureHow you get itWhat it mostly gives
DwellingBuilt on a hex of your colourWorkers, the base currency
Trading houseUpgrade a dwellingCoins and power
TempleUpgrade a trading housePriests, and a favour tile
StrongholdUpgrade a trading houseYour faction's special ability
SanctuaryUpgrade a templePriests, and a favour tile

Notice what upgrading really does: the dwelling piece goes back onto your board, covering its worker income again, and a trading house comes off instead. Upgrading trades one kind of income for another, it does not stack them. A tableau of nothing but trading houses is a player with plenty of coins and nobody to do the work.

Three buildings matter beyond their income. Temples and sanctuaries each hand you a favour tile, which is a small permanent bonus plus a step up a cult track. The stronghold turns on the ability that makes your faction that faction, and it is usually the single most important build in your game. And every structure has a power value, one for a dwelling, two for most of the rest, three for stronghold and sanctuary, which is the number everything in points 6, 7 and 9 is counted in.

The most common first-game shape

Players build two dwellings, upgrade them both, and stall in round three with four coins and one worker. Dwellings are cheap and they make workers. Build more of them than feels elegant before you start upgrading.

Remember this

Your board is a machine that pays out once a round for six rounds. A dwelling built in round one pays five more times; one built in round five pays once.

5 You can only build where you already are

The map is the clock, and it runs out faster than the rounds do.

You cannot terraform or build just anywhere. A hex has to be adjacent to something you already own, either directly touching one of your structures or reachable across water by your shipping level, which is a track you can upgrade a few times over the game. Your first two dwellings are placed before the game starts, and everything you ever build grows out of those two seeds.

This is the constraint that turns a pleasant economic game into a knife fight. There are only so many hexes of each colour, they are in fixed places, and once someone builds on one it is gone for good. Nobody can remove a building in Terra Mystica. Every structure placed on the map is permanent.

So the map asks a question every round that the rules never state out loud: which direction am I growing, and who is growing towards me? A player who builds in a tidy blob is easy to cut off. A player who reaches out along a line keeps options but pays more spades. Both are defensible. Building with no plan at all is not.

Remember this

Look at the hex you want two rounds before you need it. If a rival can reach it before you can, it is already theirs, and you should want a different hex.

6 Power is not produced, it circulates

The strangest rule in the game, and the one worth learning first.

You have twelve power tokens spread across three bowls. They start mostly in bowl I. Power is never created or destroyed, it only moves: bowl I to bowl II to bowl III, always in that direction.

When you "gain power", nothing new arrives. You move that many tokens one bowl to the right. When you spend power, you take tokens out of bowl III and drop them all the way back to bowl I, where they have to walk round again.

The consequence is easy to say and hard to feel: every point of power you spend costs you two points of power gained. A token has to make two hops to become useful. When somebody offers you four power it is not four actions of fuel, it is more like two.

What is power for? Power actions on the main board: extra spades, a free bridge, a lump of coins or workers or a priest. They are shared, so the first player to reach for one each round gets it. Power is also the number your buildings are measured in for towns and for what your neighbours receive, which is the next point.

You may also burn power: throw away one token from bowl II to move another from II to III. It is a real cost, and it is also how you reach a power action one turn earlier than anybody expects.

Remember this

Power is a queue, not a pile. Counting how much you have means counting what is in bowl III, not what is on the table.

7 Your neighbours pay you, and you pay for it

The only real conversation in the game is: do you want this, and is it worth a point?

When a player builds or upgrades a structure, every other player with structures adjacent to that hex is offered power equal to the total power value of their adjacent buildings. You may take all of it or none of it. And if you take it, you pay one victory point for every power gained beyond the first. Three power costs two points.

This single rule does most of the work of making Terra Mystica a game rather than a puzzle. It means:

  • Building next to a rival is a real decision. You get a cheaper trading house when you do it, and you hand them fuel. Both are true at once.
  • Points are a currency you can buy with. Early on, power is worth more than points, because power taken in round one gets spent five more times. Late on, the reverse.
  • Sitting alone is not safe, it is quiet. A player in an empty corner of the map gets no offers at all, and has to generate everything themselves.
How it actually plays

Round one, a neighbour upgrades a dwelling next to your two structures. You are offered three power for two points.

Take it. Three power is well over half a power action, and a power action in round one is worth a great deal more than two points. Now round six: the same offer costs the same two points, and the power will never be spent.

Early: almost always take it. Late: almost always decline.

Remember this

The question is never "can I afford the points". It is "will I spend this power before the game ends".

8 The cults

Four ladders on the side of the board that pay you twice.

Fire, water, earth and air each have a track running from zero up to ten. Climbing them does two things: passing certain steps hands you power immediately, and at the end of the game the players highest on each track score victory points, eight for first place down to two for third.

There are two ways up. Favour tiles, taken whenever you build a temple or sanctuary, move you up a track straight away and often keep paying for the rest of the game. And you can send a priest to a cult, which is a whole action and costs you a priest you will not see again for a long while.

Priests are the scarcest resource in the game. You have seven of them ever, your income gives them out slowly, and every one you post to a cult is one not spent on something else. That is the tension: cults are real points, and they are paid for in the thing you have least of.

The step you cannot take

You can never move to space 10 on a cult without spending a town key, and keys come almost exclusively from founding towns. Players who plan a cult win without a town in sight find themselves stuck on 9 with nowhere to go.

The healthy way to hold cults in your head: they are not a strategy on their own, they are a tiebreaker with an income attached. Getting to the top of one track you were already climbing is excellent. Fighting for all four is how you finish with a beautiful cult board and eleven buildings on the map.

Remember this

Cult points are cheap when they fall out of buildings you wanted anyway. They are expensive when you buy them with priests.

9 Towns

The one moment in the game where a normal build turns into a windfall.

Look at a connected group of your own structures. Add up their power values, one per dwelling, two per trading house or temple, three per stronghold or sanctuary. When a connected group reaches seven power value and at least four buildings, you found a town and immediately take a town tile.

A town tile is a large one-off payment: points plus a resource lump, and crucially a town key, the thing that unlocks the top step of a cult track. Most winning games contain two or three towns. Most first games contain none, because the player never counted.

So towns reward a specific habit: before you place a dwelling, ask whether there is a placement that does the same job while completing a cluster. Often there is, and it is worth several turns of income. The stronghold and sanctuary carry three power each, which is why building one so often turns a quiet corner into a town on the spot.

Remember this

Count your clusters every single round. Seven power and four buildings is the number that turns an ordinary turn into your best turn.

10 The six scoring tiles

Read all six before you take your first action. This is the habit that wins games.

Six scoring tiles are laid out at setup, one per round, face up. Each says what earns bonus points in that round: two points per dwelling built, three per trading house, five per town founded, points for spades or for priests sent to cults. And each one also pays a resource at the end of that round for standing on a particular cult track.

Those tiles are not decoration. Over a game they are worth twenty to thirty points, and they are the only part of the game that tells you when to do things. The map tells you where and the wheel tells you how much. The tiles tell you when.

What planning around them looks like

The round four tile pays five points per town. So in round three you build up to six power value in two different clusters and stop, leaving each one build short.

In round four you complete both. The buildings were on your list anyway, and you have collected ten bonus points for doing them on a Thursday instead of a Wednesday.

Same buildings, same cost, ten more points.

The flip side is just as real: if round two pays for trading houses and you spend round two terraforming, you did not lose points exactly, but everyone who read the tiles gained them. In a game decided by single digits that is the whole margin.

Remember this

Everyone builds roughly the same things over six rounds. The winner built them in the order the tiles were paying for.

11 Bonus cards and passing

A small decision at the end of every round that quietly sets up the next one.

When you pass, you hand back the bonus card you were holding and take a new one from the small face-up row. Each card gives a modest income, sometimes a special action, sometimes end-of-round points for building a particular thing. There is always exactly one more card than there are players, so the last player to pass has no choice at all.

That turns passing into a three-part decision, and new players usually only see the first part.

  • Have I run out of useful actions? The obvious one.
  • Which card do I want for next round? Cards that pay for the coming scoring tile are worth passing a turn early to secure.
  • Where do I want to be in turn order? First to pass is first to act next round, which is how you get the hex or the power action you are worried about losing.

Passing first when you still have resources feels like quitting. It is not: it is buying first pick of the map and first pick of the cards, and paying for it with the tail end of a round where everyone else is also out of money.

Remember this

Passing is a move, not the absence of one. Decide what you are buying with it before you do it.

12 Your faction is a plan, not a paint job

Fourteen peoples, and each one is genuinely trying to do something different.

Every faction has its own income, its own starting position on the cult tracks, its own costs, and a stronghold ability that changes what its good turns look like. A short sample gives the range:

FactionThe ideaWhat it makes you do
WitchesA free dwelling on any forest hex once the stronghold is upSprawl fast and far, take towns other people cannot reach
NomadsTurn a neighbouring hex into desert without spadesExpand sideways for nothing, early and often
Chaos MagiciansStart with one dwelling instead of two; double-action strongholdBegin slow, then take huge turns nobody can match
EngineersCheap everything, and bridgesGrind out a small tidy network and score cults and towns
SwarmlingsExpensive buildings, enormous town bonusesBuild few, big, connected clusters and cash them in

Two things follow. First, your stronghold is usually the most important build in your game, because it is the moment your faction starts playing its own game instead of a generic one, and every round after it is a round it pays. Getting it up in round two or three rather than round four is often the whole difference.

Second, a faction tells you which of the systems in this briefing you should care about most. The Witches care about the map. The Engineers care about cults and towns. Nobody does everything well, and trying to is the most expensive mistake available.

Remember this

Read your faction board as an instruction, not a bonus. It is telling you what your cheap moves are, and cheap moves are the game.

13 How the game ends, and what it counts

Round six is not a round. It is a controlled demolition.

After the sixth round, three things score, and only one of them is the one people watch:

  • The largest connected network. Eighteen, twelve and six points for the biggest connected chain of your structures. This is enormous and it is decided by hexes you placed in rounds two and three.
  • The cult tracks. Eight, four and two on each of four tracks, with ties splitting.
  • Leftover resources. Every three coins is a point, and workers and priests convert to coins first.

That last line is why the final round looks so strange to a newcomer. Everyone spends everything. Resources left in hand are worth a third of a point each at best, so the correct round six is to convert every worker, coin, priest and drop of power into buildings, spades and cult steps, and end the game with an empty board. Holding six workers at the end is throwing away two points for no reason.

The network score in particular deserves attention early. It rewards a single chain, not a total count, so a player with nine buildings in two separate groups scores worse than one with seven in a row. Bridges and shipping join groups together, and joining two groups in round six with one build is one of the classic large swings.

Remember this

Spend to zero. And know by round four whether your buildings form one chain or two, because you can still fix it then.

14 How not to lose your first game

The five instincts that cost beginners the most.

Nothing in Terra Mystica punishes you loudly. There is no attack, no bankruptcy, no moment where the table tells you it went wrong. You simply find, somewhere in round four, that you have nothing to do and no money to do it with. These are the five reasons.

You upgraded too early

Trading houses and temples are satisfying and they stop your workers coming in. A round-one dwelling pays you for five rounds; the trading house it becomes pays coins you have nothing to spend on. Build wide first, upgrade when you have the workers to burn.

You declined power because it cost points

In round one, three power for two points is a bargain, and beginners turn it down all day because points feel precious and power feels abstract. Points are the score, but power taken early gets spent four or five times. Take it early, refuse it late.

You never read the scoring tiles

Twenty-plus points sit in those six tiles and they are face up from the start. Doing the same work in the round that pays for it is free money, and it is the clearest gap between a first game and a fifth one.

You never counted to seven

Four buildings, seven power value, a town tile and a key. Players go entire games without noticing they were one dwelling away, twice. Count your clusters before every build.

You saved your resources

There is no prize for a tidy supply. Workers in hand do nothing, priests in hand do nothing, and power in bowl III at the end of round six is worth exactly zero. If you cannot see what you are saving for, you are not saving, you are losing slowly.

The whole game in one line

Six rounds, one action at a time, on a map that runs out before the rounds do. Build wide early, take the power, count to seven, and do each thing in the round that pays for it.
