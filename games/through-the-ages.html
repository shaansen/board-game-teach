A learner's field guide

Through
the Ages 6,000 years of civilization, one small yellow cube at a time.

You start with four people who have not invented the wheel. You finish with people running nuclear power plants. This guide walks the whole game, one idea per card: no rulebook voice, no memorising.

01 The big picture

It looks like a war game. It's actually a jobs game.

Open the box and you see armies, wonders, kings, and a card called Bombers. Everyone's first instinct is: okay, I conquer people.

Nope. In a normal 4-player game of Through the Ages, most players never fight a single war. What you actually spend two hours doing is deciding who in your civilization does what job.

Every person in your entire empire is one little yellow cube. That's the whole secret.

Follow the cube

You have a small pile of yellow cubes called your population bank. That pile is your unborn future citizens. To turn one into a real live person, you feed it: you pay food. Now it's an available worker, standing around doing nothing.

Then you give it a job by placing it on a card:

A farmer, a miner, a scientist, a priest and a soldier are the exact same yellow cube. The only difference is which card it's standing on. That means every turn you're really answering two questions:

  • Can I afford to bring a new person into the world? (food)
  • What job do I put them in? (and is that still the right job in 30 minutes?)
The classic beginner mistake

New players build the shiniest cards and forget they have nobody left to work in them. A gorgeous Library with no cube on it produces zero science. The card is just an empty job opening.

The two halves of the table

There are two decks, and they are different colours on purpose:

Here's why the red half matters even if you hate fighting: if your army is the weakest at the table, other players can play a red card and simply take your stuff: steal resources, steal science, steal a worker. You don't have to win wars. You just have to not be the obvious victim.

And how do you actually win?

One number. Culture points. Not land, not army size, not wonders; those are all just tools. At the end of Age III, whoever has the most culture wins, full stop. Every decision you make is secretly the question: does this eventually turn into culture?

Remember this

Yellow cube = a person. Blue cube = stuff (food or resources). The game is deciding who does what, and then, near the end, cashing all of it in for culture.

02 The win condition

Culture is the only number that matters, and it drips.

There is one scoring track, and it is culture. Everything else on your board is machinery for producing it.

The important word is drips. Culture is not a lump you grab at the end. Almost all of it arrives the same way food and science do: a little bit every single turn, from temples, from finished wonders, from your leader, from your government. At the end of your turn you look at your culture rate, say "4", and move your marker four spaces. Then you do it again next turn. And again.

A temple built on turn 4 pays you every turn for the rest of the game. A temple built on turn 16 pays you twice.

This single fact explains almost every strategic argument in Through the Ages. Points built early are worth several times the same points built late, which is why experienced players seem oddly relaxed about scoring nothing for the first half hour: they are building the thing that will print culture for twenty turns.

Science is the same shape

Science works exactly like culture: a per-turn drip you spend on cards. So does food, so do resources. Your civilization is really four little taps, and your job is to decide how wide each tap is opened:

  • Food, which buys new people
  • Resources, which buy buildings, units and wonders
  • Science, which buys the right to have better cards at all
  • Culture, which buys nothing and wins the game
The trap

Ending the game with a huge pile of unspent science and resources feels like wealth. It scores nothing. Leftovers are just proof you built the engine and never turned it on.

Remember this

Culture arrives per turn, so the real currency is turns of ownership. Ask not "how many points is this?" but "how many turns will this be paying me?"

03 Your turn

Four actions per century. It is never, ever enough.

On your turn you get a handful of little white tokens: your civil actions. At the start you have four. Each thing you want to do costs one, sometimes more:

  • Take a card from the row: 1, 2 or 3 actions depending on how fresh it is
  • Increase population (turn a cube from the bank into a worker): 1
  • Build a building or a military unit, or add a stage to a wonder: 1
  • Play a civil card from your hand (a technology, a leader, a government): 1
  • Upgrade a worker from an old building to a newer one: 1

Four actions. That's the whole century. Unused actions are simply lost at end of turn; you cannot bank them.

Nothing in this game is a bad move in isolation. Everything is a bad move relative to the thing you didn't do instead. Once you feel that, you are playing properly.

You also get a second, smaller budget of red military actions (two, at the start). Those are a separate wallet that pays for military things only: drawing military cards, raising units, playing tactics. You cannot spend military actions on a farm, and you cannot spend civil actions on a war. We will come back to the red wallet in point 14.

Remember this

Two wallets, both tiny, neither refundable: white for building your civilization, red for defending it. Every turn you leave good moves on the table, and that is the intended feeling.

04 Where cards come from

The card row: a conveyor belt everyone is fighting over.

You do not draw civil cards. There is a row of thirteen face-up card slots down the middle of the table, and everybody takes from the same row. New cards enter at the far right end and shuffle leftwards each round; when they fall off the left end they are gone.

The clever bit is the price. Taking a card costs civil actions based on where it sits: the fresh cards on the right cost three actions, the middle costs two, and the tired old cards on the left cost one.

So the row is a live auction paid in tempo. When a card you desperately want appears at the expensive end, you have a genuinely painful choice: spend three of your four actions now, or gamble that it survives one more lap of the table and gets cheaper.

It usually does not survive. Not because your opponents want it, but because they can see that you want it.

The trap

Hoovering up cheap cards on the left because they are "good value". A card in your hand does nothing at all: you still have to spend another action to play it, and resources to build it. Six cards in hand and no engine is the most common losing position in the game.

Remember this

Every card costs you twice: once to take it, once to play it. Take only what you have the actions to actually use.

05 Growth

Each new person costs more than the last one did.

Your population bank sits in a column of slots on your board, and it is the most quietly brilliant piece of design in the box. To make a new worker you spend food equal to the number printed under the next slot, and those numbers climb: two, then two, then three, then four, then five, then seven.

You are not just paying for a person. You are paying today's price for the tenth person, and it is not the price you paid for the third.

Here is the consequence that catches everyone. A big civilization is not automatically a good one. Every worker you create eats, permanently, whether or not it has a job. If you grow faster than your farms, you spend the rest of the game feeding people who produce nothing, and a food shortage costs you culture directly.

Population is not a score. It is a mortgage.
Remember this

Grow when you have a job waiting and the food to sustain it. A worker with no job is the single most expensive object in Through the Ages.

06 The bills

Two invoices arrive every turn: hunger and corruption.

At the end of your turn, after you have produced everything, the board takes some of it back.

Bill one: food

You pay food equal to your consumption, which is driven by how many cubes have left your population bank. Cannot pay? You take a food shortage: you lose culture, and it hurts.

Bill two: corruption

Your blue resource cubes live in their own bank on your board, and it works the same way in reverse: the more resources you are hoarding, the more slots are uncovered, and the more resources you pay in corruption every turn.

Read that again, because it is the rule that makes this game feel alive. Sitting on a pile of resources is actively punished. There is no "save up quietly for ten turns" strategy, because the pile taxes itself.

The trap

Stockpiling for a big Age III wonder. By the time you get there, corruption has quietly eaten more than the wonder is worth. Spend down to a working balance and keep the pile small.

Remember this

Both bills are self-inflicted: one for having too many people, one for having too much stuff. Through the Ages taxes size, not activity, which is why the game rewards spending.

07 Buildings

A card is planning permission. A cube is the actual building.

Getting a farm running takes three separate things, and beginners routinely stop after the first:

  • Take the card from the row (costs civil actions)
  • Play the card by paying science (costs an action, and now the technology exists in your civilization)
  • Build it by paying resources and moving a yellow cube onto it (costs another action, and only now does it produce anything)

Three actions, two currencies, one working farm. That is why four actions a turn feels like nothing.

Buildings come in two families, and the difference matters:

FamilyExamplesWhat to know
Farms and minesAgriculture, Irrigation, Bronze, Iron, CoalUnlimited copies. These are your economy.
Urban buildingsTemple, Library, Theatre, ArenaCapped. Your government sets how many of each type you may have.

That cap is real and it bites early. Under your starting government you can have very few of each urban building, so "I will just build six temples" is not a plan; it is a rule violation. Getting more temples means getting a better government first, which is point 11.

Remember this

Card, science, resources, worker. Miss any one of the four and the building is scenery.

08 Science

Science does not build anything. It buys the right to build.

New players treat science as a nice-to-have and then wonder why, at hour two, everybody else has cards they cannot even play.

Science is permission. Every technology card has a science price at the top, and that price is what you pay just to put the card into play. The resources come later and separately. Without science, the good cards sit in your hand as decoration.

And science prices scale brutally across the game. An Age I technology might cost a handful. An Age III one costs several times that. If your science tap is still a trickle when Age III arrives, you are locked out of the entire second half of the card pool while your opponents upgrade around you.

Science is the only resource whose shortage you notice twenty minutes after it was too late to fix.

The classic dilemma

A cube in a library makes science and no food. A cube in a farm makes food and no science. Early on you barely have cubes at all, so the first library genuinely means one fewer farmer, and you will feel it. Take the hit anyway: a science engine started in Age I is compounding by Age II, and one started in Age II never catches up.

Remember this

Science is not points and it is not stuff. It is access. Fall behind on it and the game keeps dealing you cards you are not allowed to use.

09 Upgrading

You do not add better buildings. You move people into them.

You have two farmers on Agriculture, each producing one food. You research Irrigation, which produces two. What now?

You do not build a fresh Irrigation farm with a brand new worker, because a brand new worker costs food, raises your consumption forever, and you probably do not have a spare cube anyway. Instead you upgrade: pay the difference in resources, pick a cube up off the old card, and set it down on the new one. Same person, better tools.

Two things follow from this. First, an old card with nobody on it is fine; leave it there, it is a ladder rung you have climbed past. Second, and less obvious: your board naturally wants to stay small and modern, because every extra person is a permanent food bill while every upgrade is a one-off resource payment.

The trap

Building the new mine and hiring a new worker for it, every time. Do that three times and your consumption has outrun your farms while your opponents did the same work with the cubes they already had.

Remember this

Ask "can I move someone?" before "can I hire someone?" Upgrading buys output without buying rent.

10 Happiness

Unhappy civilizations do not go to war. They stop showing up.

There is a little yellow smiling face on some of your cards, and it is a hard requirement, not a bonus.

The rule: the more workers you have out in the world, the more happiness you must supply. Temples supply it. Some wonders supply it. Some leaders and governments supply it. If your happiness is short of what your population demands, your civilization goes into uprising, and an uprising is not a scolding. Depending on how short you are, some or all of your production simply does not happen that turn, and you can lose culture on top.

It is the only rule in the game that can zero you out in one turn, and it is entirely predictable.

The practical version: before you press the button on a new worker, glance at your happiness line. Growth is what pushes you over the edge, so the temple that keeps you legal has to be bought before the population that needs it, not after.

The happy accident is that temples produce culture too. The thing that keeps your people working is the same thing that wins you the game, which is the tidiest bit of design in the box.

Remember this

Happiness is a licence to operate, checked every turn. Buy it one step ahead of your population, never one step behind.

11 Government

Your action budget is itself a card you can replace.

Remember the four white actions from point 3? Those come from your government card, and it is sitting right there on your board, upgradeable.

You start under Despotism: few civil actions, few military actions, tight urban building limits. Better governments give more of all three. Democracy is a different game from Despotism, not a slightly better one, because two extra actions per turn across twenty turns is forty extra actions.

Government doesWhich controls
Civil actionsHow much you can do per turn
Military actionsHow much red-deck business you can do
Urban building limitHow many temples, libraries, theatres you may own

Two ways to change it

Research it, like any technology: take the card, pay a large pile of science, play it. Expensive but calm.

Revolt: stage a revolution instead, which is cheaper in science but consumes your whole turn's civil actions. You get the new government and you get nothing else that turn.

Both hurt. That is intentional: this is the biggest upgrade available to you, so the game makes you pay for it with the thing it is about to give you more of.

The trap

Waiting until you can afford a government "comfortably". You never can. Meanwhile the player who took the pain in Age I has been playing with more actions per turn than you for an hour, and that gap does not close.

Remember this

A government upgrade is not a card, it is a bigger turn, every turn, forever. Price it that way.

12 Wonders

Paid in instalments, and an unfinished one pays nothing.

Wonders are the most seductive cards in the game and the most common way to lose with them.

A wonder has a row of costs printed on it, for example 2, 3, 3. Each civil action you spend on it pays off one of those instalments, using resources you have that turn. So the wonder in the example needs three separate actions, spread over three separate turns, and roughly eight resources.

While it is half built, a wonder gives you nothing at all. No culture, no ability, no consolation. It is a hole in your board that you keep pouring resources into, and if the game ends before you finish it, that was simply money set on fire.

Worse, you can only work on one wonder at a time, so a stalled wonder blocks the next one.

What makes them worth it anyway: a finished wonder needs no worker. It is production without a mouth to feed, which is exactly what your food-constrained civilization wants. And the good ones bend a rule permanently: extra happiness, extra military strength, cheaper technologies.

Remember this

Start a wonder only when you can see the last instalment from where you stand. Half a wonder is worth exactly nothing, and it is blocking the next one.

13 Leaders

One famous person at a time, breaking one rule for you.

Leaders are the cheapest big swing in the game: no resources, no worker, just an action and sometimes a bit of science. You may have exactly one in play, and playing a new one retires the old one immediately.

Each leader breaks a rule in your favour. One makes your wonders cheaper. One turns your military strength into culture. One gives you extra happiness so you can grow. One doubles what a category of building produces.

Because they cost so little, the real question with a leader is not "is this good?" but "does this match the civilization I already have?" A leader who rewards wonders is worthless if you are not building wonders. A leader is a multiplier, and a multiplier needs something to multiply.

The trap

Hoarding a strong Age II leader for the perfect moment. Leaders age out with their era: when the age turns over, an unplayed leader in your hand is just a dead card. Play them early, play them for their whole lifespan.

Remember this

A leader is a rule-breaker on a timer. Get them onto the table while the timer still has turns on it.

14 The red wallet

Military actions, and the hand nobody can see.

Now the other half of the table. Your red military actions (two, at the start, more from better governments) buy three things:

  • Drawing military cards from the face-down deck into your hand
  • Building units: warriors, riders, knights, and later on, tanks and air forces, each of which needs a worker and resources like any building
  • Playing tactics: a card that says which formation of units is currently fashionable, doubling the value of an army arranged that way

The important structural fact is the hidden hand. Civil cards are public: everyone sees what you took. Military cards are secret. Nobody knows whether the quiet player with two military actions spare is holding a war declaration or three blanks.

Half the military game is played by people who never attack. The threat does the work.

Which is also the trap in the other direction: military cards in hand are not culture. Every action spent drawing red cards is an action not spent on your economy, and a hand full of aggressions you never dare play is just an expensive bluff you paid full price for.

Remember this

Red actions and white actions come from the same government card but cannot be swapped. Deciding how militarised you are is a decision you make once, structurally, not fresh each turn.

15 Strength

Your army is one public number, and it is a shield, not a sword.

Add up your units and your bonuses and you get a single figure: your military strength. It sits on a track everyone can read, next to everyone else's.

Almost every hostile card in the game resolves by comparing that number. Aggressions can only be aimed at players weaker than you. Wars are won by the bigger number. Events routinely punish whoever is lowest and reward whoever is highest.

So the useful way to read the track is not "am I strong?" but "am I last?".

This is why the standard advice is keep pace, do not lead. Being the strongest army is expensive: every unit is a worker who could have been a scientist, plus resources, plus a permanent food bill. Being the weakest is more expensive still, because the other three players will help themselves to your civilization all game.

Cheap ways to move the number without militarising your whole economy:

  • Upgrade the units you already have, rather than raising new ones
  • Play a tactics card, which can double an army you already paid for
  • Take a wonder or leader that grants strength directly, with no worker attached
Remember this

Military strength is insurance. Buy enough that nobody chooses you, then stop and go back to the real game.

16 Getting robbed

Aggressions and wars: the two speeds of unpleasantness.

You have a political move available once per turn, and this is where most of it happens.

Aggressions: instant, small, humiliating

An aggression is played, compared, resolved, done. If your strength beats the target's, you take something: a few resources, some science, a worker, a chunk of culture. The victim gets no turn to react. They can only reduce the damage by having had a bigger army before you played the card.

The damage is rarely fatal by itself. Being the table's designated victim, three or four times, absolutely is.

Wars: slow, telegraphed, enormous

A war is declared now and resolved on your next turn. In between, the defender knows it is coming and can scramble: build units, play tactics, upgrade, panic. Then the strengths are compared and the loser takes a serious hit, the kind that decides games.

An aggression is a mugging. A war is an appointment for a mugging, which is somehow worse.

You also do not need cards to feel this. Ordinary events, drawn by anyone, regularly hand out rewards to the strongest and penalties to the weakest player. The military track is being scored quietly all game, whether or not anyone declares anything.

The trap

Retaliating. Someone raids you, you spend three turns building an army to punish them, and a fourth player who stayed out of it wins by twenty culture. Damage that has already happened is sunk. Ask only whether the next spend is the best available, never whether it is deserved.

Remember this

You cannot be attacked from above your strength. Everything hostile in this game flows downhill, so all you have to do is not be the bottom of the hill.

17 Events

You are stocking a deck of surprises for your future self.

This mechanism confuses everyone at first, and it is worth the two minutes because it is the cleverest thing in the box.

There are two event piles: a future pile and a current pile. When you play an event card from your hand, it does not happen. It goes face down onto the future pile. Later, when the current pile runs dry, the whole future pile is shuffled and becomes the new current pile, and those events start coming out one per round, hitting everybody.

So you are loading the weather. If you seed a famine event while you are sitting on a food surplus, you are not being random: you are buying a punishment aimed at everyone who is not you. The player who reads an event, seeds it, and quietly prepares for it two turns later is doing something the others cannot see and cannot easily counter.

Events run the housekeeping too: the pace of new cards, the raids on the weakest army, the end-of-age bookkeeping. Ignoring the pile is a choice to let other people set the climate you live in.

Remember this

Events are the one place where you have private knowledge of the future. Seed what suits you, and remember what you seeded.

18 The clock

Four ages, and the deck is the timer.

The game runs through Age A (the tiny setup age), then Ages I, II and III. Each age has its own civil and military decks, and they get progressively stronger and progressively more expensive in science.

Nobody announces the end. The card decks are the clock: when an age's deck runs out, the next one starts feeding the row, and when the last age's deck is exhausted, the game finishes shortly after. In practice this means the pace of the table controls the length of the game, and you can feel the end approaching by watching how thin the deck has become.

Three consequences a first-timer should hold onto:

  • Costs jump at each boundary. The technology that felt affordable in Age I has a big brother in Age II that costs twice the science. If your science rate stalled, the age change is when you find out.
  • Cards expire. When the age turns, remaining cards of the old age get cleared out of the row and out of relevance. That leader you were saving became scenery.
  • The last age is short. By the time Age III cards arrive, you have only a handful of turns left. Anything that takes four turns to pay back is now a loss.
Age I is where you buy your engine. Age II is where you run it. Age III is where you sell it for culture.
Remember this

The decks are the timer, so count them. Knowing you have five turns left changes every decision on your board, and the information is sitting in plain sight.

19 Scoring

Where the culture actually comes from, in the end.

At the end, you count what you have been collecting all along, plus a small amount of last-minute arithmetic. It is worth knowing the shape of the final total before your first game, because it tells you what to build:

SourceWhen it paysNotes
Temples, theatres, other culture buildingsEvery turnNeeds a worker on it, and happiness to keep them working
Finished wondersEvery turn, plus a lump when finishedNo worker needed, which is why they are precious
Your leaderEvery turn, some of themOnly while they are your current leader
Government and pactsEvery turn, some of themQuiet, steady, easy to forget
Wars, aggressions, coloniesOne-offCulture taken directly from someone else, so it swings twice
End-game bonusesOnce, at the endCards that score per building, per unit, per level of science
Leftover science, resources, foodNeverZero. It scores nothing at all.

The pattern is unmissable: the big money is in the rows that say every turn. A player who builds two temples in Age I and quietly runs them for the whole game usually beats the player who assembles an impressive Age III civilization that only starts producing on the final turn.

That last row deserves its own sentence, because everyone learns it the hard way. Your final hand of unplayed cards, your neat stack of blue cubes, your carefully hoarded science: nothing. Spend it, or explain to yourself afterwards why you didn't.

Remember this

Score the per-turn rows early and the one-off rows late. In the last two turns, convert anything convertible: unspent is unscored.

20 Your first game

How not to lose it: five instincts to switch off.

Every one of these feels correct. Every one of these is why first games end at half the winner's score.

1. "I'll build a big civilization."

No. Build a small, dense, modern one. Every extra worker is a permanent food bill and a permanent happiness demand. Growth is only good when a job and the food to sustain it are already waiting. When in doubt, upgrade someone instead of hiring someone.

2. "I'll take all the good cards now and use them later."

A hand of cards is a hand of debts: each one still needs an action, science, resources, and often a worker. Take a card when you can see the turn you will play it on. Two cards played beat five cards held, every time.

3. "I'll stay peaceful and out of trouble."

Peaceful is a fine plan. Weakest is not. Those are different things, and the difference is roughly one unit every age. Look at the military track every single turn and ask one question: am I last? If you are, fix it before you do anything clever.

4. "I'll get the economy sorted, then start on culture."

There is no "then". Culture pays per turn, so the temple you delay by six turns loses six turns of scoring you can never recover. Get something producing culture in Age I even if it feels premature, and never have zero culture production on the board.

5. "I'll save up for something big."

Corruption taxes the pile, unspent resources score nothing, and the deck is running out faster than you think. Through the Ages rewards the player whose stuff is always deployed, always working, always a little uncomfortable.

Small, busy, boringly consistent, and never last in the army. That is the winning civilization, and it looks nothing like an empire.

The one-line version of the whole game

You have four actions and about twenty turns. Turn cubes into jobs, keep your people fed and content, stay off the bottom of the military track, and start the culture drip earlier than feels sensible. Everything else in these twenty points is detail hanging off that sentence.

Remember this

You will lose your first game. Lose it while watching your action count and your culture rate, and your second game will be a completely different experience.
