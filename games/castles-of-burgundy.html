Learn it properly · 14 points

The Castles ofBurgundy

A honeycomb of thirty-seven empty spaces in front of you, two dice a turn, and twenty-five turns to fill it. Everything about this game is gentle, polite and quietly ruthless.

1 The big picture

You are filling in a map, and every tile you lay pays you twice.

Castles of Burgundy is a fifteenth century Loire valley in the fiction and a very tight efficiency puzzle in practice. In front of you is your estate: a honeycomb of hex spaces grouped into coloured regions. Rivers are blue, cities are beige, pastures are light green, mines grey, castles dark green, knowledge yellow. Every space also carries a number from 1 to 6.

You take hex tiles from a shared board and lay them into that honeycomb. Two things then happen, and understanding both is understanding the game. First, the tile does something the instant it lands: a ship fetches you goods, a pasture pays out points, a building does you a favour. Second, when a tile fills the last empty space of its coloured region, the whole region scores, and it scores far more than any single tile ever did.

So a good turn is never just "I placed a thing". It is "I placed a thing, it did a job on the way in, and it brought a region one space closer to paying out."

Nobody attacks you and nobody can block your board. The competition is for the tiles in the shared shop, for turn order, and for the bonuses that go to whoever finishes a colour first. Everything else is you against your own inefficiency.

Remember this

Every tile pays twice: once when it lands, once when it completes its region. The second payment is the bigger one.

2 The two boards

One board is the shop and the clock. The other one is you.

The board in the middle of the table is not a map and nothing moves across it. It is a shop: six numbered depots, each holding a handful of face-up tiles and a pile of goods. The number on a depot is the die face you need to buy from it. Around the edge that same board keeps the time, the turn order and the score, which is why it looks more complicated than it is.

Your own board is the estate, plus a small clerical corner: three spaces where tiles wait before you place them, room for your goods, your coins and your workers, and a parking spot for dice you have already spent. Nothing in that corner scores. It is all working space.

Remember this

Depot numbers are die faces. Estate space numbers are die faces. The entire game is the numbers 1 to 6, used two different ways.

3 The clock

Five phases of five rounds, and the reward for finishing shrinks in every one.

The game is five phases long, each five rounds, which gives you twenty-five turns and no more. At the start of each phase the shop is wiped and restocked, so the tile you were saving up for is gone. Within a phase, everybody rolls at the same time and then plays in turn order.

The important thing is not the structure, it is the decay running through it. The bonus for completing a region is 10 points in the first phase and 2 in the last, dropping by two each time. Nothing else in this game punishes dawdling so directly. A region sitting one hex short of finished is not "nearly there", it is losing you two points a phase.

There is one small piece of design worth admiring. Each round begins with a goods tile being dealt onto the board, five per phase, so the goods still waiting to be dealt tell you exactly how many rounds are left. You never have to ask how long the game has to run. It is sitting there in front of you.

Remember this

Everyone rolls at once, so plan while other people are playing. And the longer a region stays open, the less it will ever pay you.

4 Your turn

Two dice, two actions, four things they can be.

A turn is over fast. You have two dice, each buys exactly one action, in either order, and doing the same thing twice is fine. There are only four actions in the entire game:

  • Take a tile from the depot matching your die, into your storage
  • Place a tile from storage onto an estate space matching your die
  • Sell goods of the colour matching your die
  • Take workers, which any die can do, whatever it shows

Read that list again and notice what it does to you. A tile takes two dice to get onto your board: one to buy it, one to place it. Fifty dice actions in the whole game, and every tile eats two of them. You will place roughly twenty tiles all game, so treat each one as the expensive thing it is.

Remember this

Buying and building are separate actions. A tile you cannot place is half a turn already spent and nothing to show for it.

5 Workers

The dice tell you what you rolled. The workers tell you what you meant.

This is the rule that stops a dice game from being a dice game. A worker turns one die up or down by a pip, you can spend several on the same die, and the wheel wraps, so a 6 becomes a 1 for a single worker. Rolled badly? Pay your way out of it.

The catch is the exchange rate. Workers arrive two at a time and cost a whole die to fetch, which is half a turn, and at the end of the game two leftover workers are worth a single point. So workers are not savings. They are a lubricant, and the only bad worker is one still sitting on your board when the game ends.

The trap

Finishing with a tidy pile of eight workers. That is two dice spent to earn four points. Spend them the moment they buy you a placement you actually wanted.

Remember this

Workers are how you stop apologising for your dice. They are worth far more mid-game than they will ever be worth at the end.

6 Storage

Three chairs in the waiting room, and a full room is a real problem.

Tiles you buy do not go into your estate. They go to a holding area with exactly three spaces, where they wait for a future die to match the space you want them on. Take a fourth tile and one of the old ones is thrown out of the game entirely.

Three sounds generous until you remember what a tile needs before it can leave the waiting room: the right number, on a space of the right colour, next to something you have already built. A tile that fails any of those is furniture.

The trap

Hoovering up gorgeous tiles because they were there. If you cannot point at the space a tile is going to live on, do not buy it. Every discarded tile is half a turn you paid for and set on fire.

Remember this

Buy for next turn, not for one day. Storage is a waiting room, not a collection.

7 Placing

Number, colour, neighbour. All three, every single time.

Placing is the heart of the game and it has three locks on it. The die says which numbered space you may fill. The tile's colour must match that space's colour. And the space must touch something you have already built, which at the start of the game means it must touch the castle sitting in the middle of your estate.

That third lock is the one beginners underrate. Your estate grows outward like frost across a window and it never jumps a gap, so if you build hard in one direction the far side of the board stays unreachable for hours. Once a tile is down it never moves again.

Remember this

Number, colour, neighbour. Grow in two directions early, or you will sit holding perfect tiles with nowhere legal to put them.

8 Completing regions

This is where the points are, and they leak away while you admire your board.

Fill every space of one coloured region and it scores at once, in two helpings. The first depends on how big the region was, and the curve is savage: one space is worth 1 point, but a region of eight is worth 36. The second is the phase bonus from point 3, the same for every region regardless of size, falling from 10 to 2 as the game runs out.

The trap

Spreading tiles evenly across a beautiful, balanced, half-finished estate. An unfinished region has paid you almost nothing at all. Close the small ones early and the big ones before the bonus rots.

Remember this

Big regions are worth enormously more, and every region is worth less the longer you leave it. Finish things.

9 Ships

Blue tiles buy you goods and shove you up the queue.

Place a ship and two things happen. You sweep up the goods piled on any one depot you like, which are your future money and points. Then you move up the turn order track, landing on top of whoever was there, which means you jump ahead of them.

That second half is why experienced players fight over ships. Turn order matters much more than it looks in a game where everyone shops from the same six depots: the first player sees them untouched, and by the fourth player the tile you needed is usually gone. A ship early in a phase can hand you the pick of the shop for the rest of it.

Remember this

Take the fattest pile of goods, not the tidy one. And remember that a ship is a queue jump, which in a game about a shared shop is worth real money.

10 Animals

A pasture pays for the whole herd again every time you add to it.

Light green tiles show two to four animals of one kind. Place one and you score points for the animals on it. Here is the good part: if that pasture already holds animals of the same kind, every one of those tiles scores all over again.

So pastures reward concentration, not variety. Four cows into an empty pasture is a modest four points. Four more cows into that same pasture pays for the new tile and the old one together. Do it a third time and you are collecting for the entire herd for the third time.

Remember this

One species per pasture, as many tiles as will fit. A mixed pasture throws most of the points away.

11 Buildings

A city is a set, not a pile.

Beige tiles are buildings, and they are the flexible, opportunistic part of the game. Each does its job the instant it is placed and none of them needs a die to fire: a bank hands you coins, a boarding house hands you workers, a watchtower simply hands you points, and three of them reach onto the shared board and pull a tile off it for free.

The rule that shapes everything is this: within one city, no two buildings may be the same type. There are eight types, so a five-space city needs five different ones. You cannot simply build the good one over and over.

Two are worth naming. The city hall places another tile out of your storage immediately, any colour, with all of its effects, which can chain into a second building and occasionally a third. The carpenter's workshop fetches another building off the board without spending a die. Both quietly break the "two dice per turn" ceiling, and that is the whole trick of a strong Burgundy turn.

Remember this

Decide which types a city will hold before you start filling it. The buildings that give you extra actions are worth more than the ones that give you things.

12 Castles, mines and knowledge

An extra action, a wage packet, and a rule you get to bend.

The three remaining colours are few in number and large in effect.

Castles, the dark green ones, hand you an extra action with a die of whatever value you choose. That is not a small favour. It is a third action in a game of two, and because you pick the number it is always exactly the placement you could not otherwise reach.

Mines, the grey ones, do nothing whatsoever when placed. They pay you a coin at the end of every phase, which means their entire value is decided by when you build them. A mine built in the first phase pays five times. A mine built in the last phase pays once and then the game ends.

Knowledge, the yellow ones, are the toy box: twenty-six of them, no two alike. Some bend a rule for the rest of the game, some improve your dice or your income permanently, and some sit there quietly and pay out at the end for things you were doing anyway. Read them when they appear, because the rule-benders change how your whole engine runs.

Remember this

Castles are extra turns and mines are a salary, so both want to be on your board early. A last-phase mine is one coin and a wasted space.

13 The three currencies

Goods, coins and workers all turn into a trickle of points, and all are worth more spent.

Goods arrive by ship and pile up in your storage by colour. Selling is one of your four actions, and it is all or nothing: the die names a colour and every tile of that colour goes at once. You get a single coin for the sale no matter how big it was, plus points for each tile. The shape of that tells you exactly how to play it. Let a colour stack up, then sell the tall pile, because the coin is a flat fee and the points are per tile.

Coins are the quiet powerhouse. Twice a turn is impossible, but once a turn you may pay two of them for a tile from the central pool without spending a die at all. That is the only place in the game where money turns straight into board position, and it is why a bank early is better than a bank late.

At the end, leftovers all convert into a thin dribble of points: a coin is one, a couple of workers is one, an unsold good is one. Tiles still sitting in storage are worth nothing at all.

The trap

Arriving at the end with a full purse and a full storage rack. Those were tiles, placements and extra actions you declined to take. The last phase is for converting, not saving.

Remember this

Everything you hold is worth about one point. Everything you spend is worth a turn.

14 Your first game

How not to lose it.

Everything in Castles of Burgundy is mild, legal and reasonable, which is exactly why beginners lose by forty points without ever spotting where it went. Five habits fix most of it.

1. Finish regions, do not decorate the estate

A half-filled estate has paid you almost nothing. Pick two or three regions, close them, then start the next. The size curve is steep and the phase bonus is draining at two points a phase while you think about it.

2. Engines belong at the start

Mines, castles and the knowledge tiles are machinery, and machinery bought late barely runs. Anything that pays out repeatedly wants to be on your board in the first two phases.

3. Never let storage clog

Three chairs, and every tile needs a specific number on a specific colour beside something you already own. If you cannot name the space it is going to, do not take it.

4. Spend the coins and spend the workers

Held to the end they are worth a point each, or less. Spent mid-game, two coins are a whole tile and three workers are any die face you like. A fat purse at the finish means you played the last hour poorer than you had to.

5. Watch the queue

Four players want the same tile out of the same depot and the last one gets what is left. Ships move you up. If you are permanently last, a ship is worth more than the tile you were saving that die for.

Remember this

Close regions, build engines early, keep the storage moving, spend what you hold, and buy your way up the queue. Efficiency is the entire game; nobody is coming to stop you.
