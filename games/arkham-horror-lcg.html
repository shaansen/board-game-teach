Miskatonic field notes · 1926

Arkham HorrorThe Card Game

A briefing for a new investigator · points 1–15

Point 01

You're not playing against anyone

There is no winner and no loser at the table. Everybody sits on the same side.

It's 1926 in Arkham, Massachusetts — a gloomy little New England town where things go bump in the night and nobody wants to talk about it. People are vanishing. Doors are opening that shouldn't open. The police have decided it's easier to look away.

You each play an investigator: one of the oddballs who doesn't look away. A jazz singer. A librarian who's read one book too many. A getaway driver with a wrench and bad decisions. You control that one character for the whole story.

So who are you playing against? The game itself. The box has a script, and the script wants you to fail. You either survive the night together, or you go down together.

The vibeFriends versus the dark.
Point 02

Your investigator card is your character sheet

Pick one investigator. That card is you for the rest of the night, and everything about how you'll survive is printed on it.

The four skills

These are the four ways to solve a problem. Every investigator is good at two and bad at two.

  • Willpower — nerve. Staring down a ghost and not running.
  • Intellect — brains. Finding the clue, reading the cursed book.
  • Combat — fists, guns, a well-swung wrench.
  • Agility — speed and reflexes. Dodging, sneaking, escaping.

A skill of 3 means you start that attempt with 3 points of effort. If the monster needs 4, you're already behind. So a 3 in Combat means you're probably fine, and a 2 in Agility means stop trying to jump out of windows.

Two ways to lose

Health drops when something bites, claws or shoots you. Sanity drops when you see something a human brain wasn't built to look at.

The cruel partEither one hitting zero takes you out. Roland has 9 health but only 5 sanity — he's tough as nails, and he can absolutely be scared to death.

The special ability

One line of text that only your investigator has. Roland's is a cop thing: shoot a monster, and the shock of it makes you spot a clue you'd missed. That single line is the whole reason you'd pick him over anyone else.

Design lessonThere is no good-at-everything investigator. Whoever you pick, you are choosing which kind of trouble you're bad at.
Point 03

Your deck is your character's stuff

Your investigator card says who you are. Your deck — 30 cards, built before the game starts — says what you brought with you.

It's your backpack, your training and your guts, shuffled together. You draw a card each turn, and what comes up decides what you can actually do about the werewolf in the parlor.

TypeLifespanThink of it as
AssetStays in play all gameAn investment — costs a turn now, pays off every turn after
EventUsed once, then discardedThe emergency button — huge power, exactly once
SkillNever played on your turnThe nnngh, try harder card

Skills are the sneaky one. You don't play them as an action at all — when you're attempting something and about to come up short, you throw them in from your hand as a last-second boost.

The core tensionYour hand is both your toolkit and your fuel. That skill card could save your life on a bad pull — or you could hold it forever and die with it unplayed.

Classes — why you can't just take the best cards

Each investigator belongs to a class, and you mostly build from your own class's cards.

  • Guardian — fighters, cops, soldiers
  • Seeker — researchers, doctors, academics
  • Rogue — chancers, thieves, gamblers
  • Mystic — spell-slingers and fortune-tellers
  • Survivor — scrappy underdogs who get by on luck

Roland the cop can't cast spells. The librarian can't buy a shotgun. That restriction is what makes each character feel like a person instead of a pile of stats.

Point 04

There's a second deck, and it's trying to kill you

In the middle of the table sits the encounter deck. You didn't build it, didn't choose it, and don't get to look at it.

At the end of every round it takes a swing at you. That moment is called the mythos phase, and it's the heartbeat of the whole game.

CardWhat it isHow it behaves
EnemyA ghoul, a cultist, a thing with too many armsAppears in your room and attaches to you. Follows you and hits you every round until you kill it or shake it off.
TreacheryA whisper in the dark, a wave of doubt, frozen fearHappens to you, then vanishes. No monster on the table. You can't fight it — you just survive it.

The encounter deck is the story

Each scenario has its own encounter deck, stuffed with the specific horrors of that night. On a train, it's derailments and things clinging to the outside of the car. In a museum, it's stolen artifacts waking up. So when you draw a card you're not just taking damage — you're turning a page.

The real pressureThat draw comes every round, no matter what. There is no turtling up and waiting until you're ready.
Point 05

The chaos bag — the moment everything hinges on

Every time you try something risky you make a skill test. And a skill test is a tiny four-step story.

There are no dice

Instead there's a small cloth bag full of cardboard tokens, and you reach in without looking and pull one out. Most of the tokens are numbers, and almost all of those numbers are negative.

What that meansThe universe is not neutral. On average, reaching into the bag makes you worse at what you were trying to do. Combat 4 against difficulty 3 is barely a coin flip — get well ahead before your hand goes in.

The special tokens

TokenWhat it does
Auto-failYou fail. Full stop — even with a skill of 40. Always in the bag, always possible.
Elder signThe one genuinely good token, and it does something different for every investigator
SymbolsSkull, cultist, tablet, elder thing — they mean whatever this scenario says they mean

The elder sign is the elegant bit. Roland gets a bonus for standing on a clue he already found; a gambler might draw cards instead. Your elder sign is a tiny printed statement about what "a lucky break" means for that specific person.

Why this beats diceA die is a machine. Reaching into a dark bag and feeling the shape of the thing in your fingers before you pull it out is a horror movie. That half-second where nobody at the table is breathing is the game working exactly as designed.
Point 06

The map is made of cards, and clues are the treasure

Location cards get laid out on the table as you discover them. That's your map, physically built from cards.

NumberWhat it isWhy you care
CluesTokens sitting on the locationEvidence, footprints, a torn page. Collecting these is how the story advances.
ShroudHow hard the place is to searchThe difficulty number for investigating there

A well-lit hallway has shroud 1. A pitch-black cellar full of cobwebs has shroud 4. Same action, wildly different odds.

Investigating

Investigate is a skill test: your Intellect against the location's shroud, chaos token and all. Succeed, and you take one clue off the location and put it on your own card. Fail, and you got nothing, the round moves on, and the encounter deck swings at you anyway.

The whole game in one lineThe clues are always somewhere inconvenient, and the person best at getting them is usually the person least able to survive being there.
Point 07

You only get three actions

Three actions total on your turn — not three of each kind. Then the next player goes, and then the encounter deck comes for everybody.

ActionWhat happensTest
MoveWalk to a connected location—
InvestigateSearch for a clue where you standIntellect vs shroud
FightSwing at a monster in your roomCombat vs fight
EvadeDodge past a monster so it can't followAgility vs evade
Play a cardPut down an asset, fire off an event—
Draw a cardPull one extra card from your deck—
Take a resourcePick up one dollar—
Use an abilityWhatever the ⟳ arrow on a card allowsvaries

Resources are money, and money is slow

Cards cost resources to play — a shotgun might cost 5. You get one resource free at the end of each round, so if you want that shotgun this decade, you're spending actions picking up dollars instead of finding clues. Early rounds are almost always a slightly frustrating shopping trip.

Evade is underrated

New players ignore it, and it's often the smartest move on the board. You don't have to kill the thing — you can get past it. Evading knocks the monster over: it stops attacking, stops following, and you walk away. Killing a big monster might take four successful attacks. Evading it takes one.

Now feel the pain

The designThree actions is never enough, and it isn't supposed to be. Every turn you're choosing which fire to leave burning. That's not a flaw — that is the game.
Point 08

Two clocks are running, and only one is yours

Next to the map sit two small decks, face up, one card of each showing at a time. This is what all those clues were for.

The act deck is your story. It says what you're trying to do right now — "find a way into the cellar" — and at the bottom it names the price, usually clues. Gather enough between everyone, hand them in, and you flip to the next act. That's you making progress.

The agenda deck is the other story: the one happening whether you show up or not. The ritual being completed. The thing waking up. Its price is doom, and doom arrives on its own — almost every round, one doom token drops onto the agenda. Nobody puts it there. Nobody can stop it. When there's enough, the agenda flips, and whatever's written on the back happens to you.

DeckFuelWhere does the fuel come from
ActCluesYou. Only you. Every clue is an action someone spent.
AgendaDoomThe game. One token most rounds, free of charge.

Why this is the whole design

Every action you spend picking up a dollar, shooting a ghoul or dodging into the next room is an action you didn't spend on clues — and the agenda doesn't care. It advances anyway.

This is also the most common way new players lose. They play carefully: kill every monster, buy every asset, keep everyone at full health. Then the agenda flips for the last time and they discover they're on act 1 of 3 with a beautifully equipped, completely doomed party.

The right instinctGo faster than feels safe. Take the risky test. Run past the monster. Accept some damage. Being a bit hurt and ahead of schedule beats being healthy and late.

So how do you actually win?

You win a scenario by finishing the last act card before the last agenda card flips. The final act almost never says "collect more clues" — it says something specific and dramatic: kill the thing, escape the building, reach the roof before sunrise. Do that, and the night ends in your favour.

OutcomeHow it happens
You winComplete the final act
You loseThe agenda deck runs out — or every investigator is defeated at once

But most scenarios don't have a simple win and loss. They have resolutions, numbered in the campaign guide, usually three or four of them. You might kill the monster and rescue the missing student. Or kill it and lose her. Or escape with your lives while it's still out there.

The honest questionNot "did we win?" but "which ending did we get?"

And then there's winning the campaign, which is the real prize. Eight scenarios later you reach the finale, and how it goes depends on everything you dragged along: how much trauma your investigators carry, which weaknesses got shuffled in, which loose ends you never tied up. You can lose individual scenarios and still win the campaign — and you can win most of them and still get a grim ending. The game measures the whole arc, not the individual nights.

The theatre of it

The back of an agenda card stays hidden until it flips. So the whole table watches doom pile up knowing something is coming, and not what. Then somebody reads it aloud, and the game you were playing five seconds ago is not the game you're playing now.

Point 09

One round, four phases — the whole machine

You now have every part. Here's what happens when they all run together.

A round is always the same four steps, in the same order, forever.

PhaseWho actsWhat happens
MythosThe gameA doom token lands on the agenda, then everyone draws an encounter card
InvestigationYouEveryone takes three actions, one player at a time
EnemyThe monstersAnything hunting you moves closer; anything adjacent attacks
UpkeepNobodyDraw one card, take one resource, stand your exhausted cards back up

Note the order on the enemy phase: you had your chance to deal with that ghoul, and you chose not to. Upkeep is the quiet bit — the deep breath before the mythos phase does it all again.

Two things worth burning into your brain

  • The game acts first and last. Mythos opens the round, the enemy phase closes it. Your three actions are squeezed in between the thing that creates problems and the thing that punishes you for not solving them.
  • Upkeep is why patience is a trap. A free card and a free dollar every round is the game whispering just wait, you'll be stronger next round. And you will be — but the agenda gets stronger for free, while you're paying for it in actions.
The honest summaryFour steps, repeating, and you only control step two.
Point 10

Losing doesn't end the story — it changes it

Everything so far has been one night. This is the part that makes Arkham Horror different from almost every other game you've played.

Going down usually isn't dying

When your health or sanity hits zero you're defeated — knocked out, dragged off, found wandering in the street at dawn. You're removed from the scenario and your friends have to finish without you. You'll be back next time.

You just won't be back whole.

Knocked out byYou takeEffect
DamagePhysical traumaMaximum health permanently −1 for the rest of the campaign
HorrorMental traumaMaximum sanity permanently −1 for the rest of the campaign

There's no healing it. No shop to visit, no rest between missions. Roland walks into game two with 4 sanity instead of 5, and he is measurably easier to break than he was last week — for every game after this one.

And if it reaches zeroThat investigator is dead. Not out for the scenario — gone. Put the card away, pick somebody new, and everyone keeps going with a stranger.

Losing a scenario doesn't stop the campaign

The campaign guide is a booklet. At the end of every scenario you turn to a section that asks what happened: did you stop the ritual? Did you flee? Did anyone die? Whatever the answer, the book has a paragraph ready for it — and the story keeps going.

  • Failed to burn down the house? The house is still standing, and three scenarios later that comes back to bite you.
  • Somebody died? Later scenarios reference the fact that they're gone.
  • Fled instead of fighting? The thing you fled from is still out there.
No resetsThe mess you make is the story you get.

That's why people get attached to a cardboard federal agent. It isn't that you might lose — it's that the version of your investigator who limps into the final scenario, down two sanity and carrying a breakdown he picked up in a museum in chapter three, is yours. Nobody else's campaign produced that exact person.

Point 11

Your investigator grows — and growth means giving something up

Point 10 was the scar. This is the other half of the same campaign.

At the end of every scenario you earn experience: for finishing acts, for killing the big named monsters (the ones with a victory value printed on them), and for how well the story resolved. A good night might be 5 XP. A rough one might be 2.

Then, before the next game, you go shopping in your own deck. And here's the crucial bit — you don't add cards, you swap them. Your deck is 30 cards at the start of the campaign and 30 cards at the end. Every upgrade is a decision about what to leave behind.

Levels and what they cost

Cards have a level, shown as little dots in the corner — 0 to 5. Everything you start with is level 0. The higher-level cards are the good stuff, and the level number is the XP price.

Way to spendCostWhat it's for
Upgrade the same cardThe difference in levelSame card, meaner version. The cheap, sensible route.
Buy something newThe full levelChanging what your character is — the librarian who buys a weapon
The fun problemYour XP is never enough. After two scenarios you'll have maybe 8 XP and a wishlist worth 25 — so you choose between one expensive card that transforms a turn, or three cheap ones that make every turn slightly less bad.

Put points 10 and 11 together

Your investigator gets better at their job and worse at surviving it, at the same time. By the final scenario Roland is carrying a genuinely terrifying deck — and about four points of trauma. He can do things in chapter eight he couldn't dream of in chapter one, and one bad chaos pull will still put him on the floor.

Not a balance problemThat's a character arc.
Point 12

Some cards in your own deck are working against you

When you build your 30 cards, you don't get to choose all 30. The game reaches in and adds weaknesses.

They do nothing good, you can never remove them, and you will absolutely draw them at the worst possible moment. You get two kinds.

KindWhere it comes fromExample
Signature weaknessWritten specifically for your investigatorRoland's Cover Up — he's a federal agent investigating things his bosses would shut down
Basic weaknessDrawn at random from a shared pileBad luck, a stalker, a crippling phobia — whatever you pull is yours for the campaign

Roland's is the most characterful card in his deck: when Cover Up appears, evidence lands on the table that he has to spend clues to bury. Clues he needed for the act deck. His own career sabotages the mission.

They go off the instant you draw them

Most weaknesses are treacheries with a nasty little rule: you don't get to hold one, plan around it or discard it. You draw during upkeep, and instead of a shotgun you've got a panic attack — right now, before you've done anything.

And it compounds. Remember point 10: consequences stick. Some of those consequences are new weakness cards permanently added to your deck. Fail badly enough and the campaign guide tells you to shuffle in a curse. Your deck creeps from 30 to 31 to 32, and the extra cards are all poison.

The strategy hiding in here

  • A bigger deck dilutes your weaknesses — 2 bad cards in 40 is safer per draw than 2 in 30.
  • A smaller deck means you see your good cards more often and your combos arrive on schedule.

Experienced players almost always take the tight 30 and accept that the bad cards come around faster. Consistency beats safety — the agenda is ticking, and a deck that reliably does its job is worth a few more panic attacks.

Why they really existIn every Lovecraft story the investigator's own history is part of the problem — the family secret, the thing they can't stop reading, the guilt they carry. Weaknesses put that in the deck. You aren't only fighting monsters out there; you're carrying a couple around with you.
Point 13

Two investigators, and the problem of being in two places

The game plays 1 to 4. Here's why the number matters more than usual.

Look back at the map in point 6: clues in the attic, the cellar and the parlor. One investigator physically cannot be in three rooms, so even with perfect play a lone investigator spends half the campaign walking.

So the party splits up. And splitting up is the most dangerous thing you can do.

Alone with a monster is a genuinely bad place to be. Enemies attach to whoever they find, and if that's the librarian with Combat 2 she can't kill it and probably can't outrun it either — while the cop with Combat 4 is two rooms away doing something useless.

Where party roles come from

Not from the rules — from the shape of that problem.

RoleUsuallyThe job
Clue-getterSeekerHigh Intellect, empties rooms of clues, is the reason the act deck ever advances, and is made of glass
FighterGuardianHigh Combat, stands between the clue-getter and everything else — and wants enemies to come after them instead
SupportRogue, Mystic, SurvivorBends toward helping one of the other two: healing, cash, extra actions, sneaking, shouting in dead languages

What makes it real teamwork

  • You can hand things over. In the same room you can pass a card or some money. The Rogue swimming in resources can fund the Guardian's shotgun — an action spent making someone else's turn better than yours.
  • Clues pool at the end. Clues sit on individual investigators, but everyone throws them in together to advance the act. It doesn't matter who found them. No scoring, no MVP — just whether the pile is big enough.
If you're starting outTwo investigators is the sweet spot. One is lonely and unforgiving; four means a blizzard of encounter cards and long gaps between turns. Two is fast, tense, and covers the map — which is why most solo players run two decks at once.
Point 14

How a monster actually works

"There's a ghoul on you" has more moving parts than it sounds. Enemy cards are built like investigator cards — same idea, pointed the other way.

The three numbers are three different questions

NumberQuestionTest
FightHow hard is it to hit?Your Combat vs this
HealthHow many hits to kill?—
EvadeHow hard is it to slip past?Your Agility vs this

Those are independent. A ghoul with Fight 3 and Evade 2 is easier to dodge than to punch; some monsters are the reverse. Reading those two numbers side by side is how you decide whether to swing or run — the single most useful habit to build.

Damage and horror are what it does to you at the end of every round it's still attached: one to health, one to sanity. A ghoul chewing on you for four rounds costs 4 health and 4 sanity, which for most investigators is close to the whole tank.

The rule that catches out every new player

When a monster is engaged with you, most actions let it hit you for free. Walk away? Free hit. Investigate? Free hit. Play a card? Free hit. It's called an attack of opportunity.

Note which actions are exemptFight and evade — the two that actually deal with the monster. That's not a coincidence. The rule exists to force a decision: handle it, or pay for pretending it isn't there.

Keywords rewrite the behaviour

  • Hunter — moves toward the nearest investigator every round. You can't leave it behind; it follows.
  • Aloof — ignores you unless you attack it. Sometimes the right answer is to walk past and let it brood.
  • Massive — engages everybody at once. No hiding behind your friend.
  • Retaliate — miss your attack, and it hits you for it.
  • Alert — fail your evade, and it hits you for that instead.

Retaliate and Alert are the mean ones because they punish the attempt. Against a Retaliate monster a low-odds swing isn't just wasteful, it's dangerous — and suddenly the safest play is to do nothing and walk away eating the attack of opportunity. A genuinely strange, genuinely Arkham kind of decision.

Point 15

How to not lose your first game

You know the rules now. Here's the stuff nobody tells you until you've already lost twice.

Almost every rookie defeat comes from the same four instincts — and all four are the instincts a normal board game trained into you.

Stop killing things

A monster with 3 health takes three successful Combat tests to remove — three actions minimum, probably four or five once the chaos bag gets involved. Evading takes one. Unless it's blocking the room you need or the scenario pays you for its corpse, walking away is usually correct. Dead monsters don't advance the act deck.

Spend your skill cards

New players clutch them like emergency money and finish the game with three in hand. But the bag pulls you down on average (point 5), so a test you enter "probably fine" is a test you lose about a third of the time — and a failed investigate costs you the action anyway. Two cards to make a test near-certain is a good trade. The card you saved for a rainier day gets shuffled away when you're defeated.

Shop fast, then stop

Yes, you need your gun. The pattern that kills people is spending rounds one to five building a perfect engine, then looking up to find the agenda on its last card. Rough rule: get your key asset out, then start earning your keep. If you haven't taken a single clue by round four, you're already behind.

Get comfortable being hurt

Health and sanity aren't a score to protect — they're a resource to spend. Taking one damage to save an action is very often a good deal, because you have 9 health and only about a dozen rounds. The party that ends at full health and loses on the agenda made a hundred small safe choices and one enormous mistake.

Two habits, not instincts

  • Read the agenda before you plan your turn. How much doom is on it, how much does it need? That number tells you whether this is a round for setting up or a round for sprinting. Most tables never look until it flips.
  • Play your first campaign on Standard, and don't be proud about it. Difficulty settings change which tokens go in the bag. Easy and Standard exist so you can learn the story without it ending in chapter two. Nobody is watching.
The mindset underneath all of itYou are not trying to survive the night. You are trying to finish the job before the night finishes you. Those sound similar, and they lead to completely different games.
