Learn the whole game · 14 points

Glass
Road

You run a glass workshop in a German forest in the 1700s. You have four seasons, fifteen workers, and two very strange dials. Here's everything, one piece at a time.

  • 1–4 players
  • 4 building periods
  • ~20 min per player
  • Uwe Rosenberg, 2013
Point 01

You make glass in the middle of a forest

The real Glass Road is a 150-mile route through the Bavarian Forest, on the German–Czech border, where people blew glass for about 700 years. Why out there in the trees? Because glass needs three things a forest hands you for free: quartz sand under the ground, wood to burn, and water to cool everything down.

You run one of those workshops. But here's the twist that decides the whole game: at the end, your glass is worth almost nothing. Only your buildings score points. Glass, bricks, wood and clay are just the currency you spend to put buildings on your land.

Locked inGlass is money. Buildings are points.
Point 02

The game is way shorter than it looks

Glass Road runs for 4 building periods (think of them as seasons). Each period is 3 card rounds. In each card round you play one card of your own.

Do the maths: 4 × 3 = 12 turns for the entire game. That's it. All those tiles and tokens in the box make it look like an epic, but it's over fast. The classic beginner mistake is spending the first two periods building a gorgeous resource engine that finally works on turn 13.

Locked in4 periods × 3 card rounds. Build early or don't build at all.
Point 03

Eight goods, and only four of them buy things

Six goods come out of the ground: quartz sand, food, charcoal, water, wood, clay. Two goods you manufacture: glass and brick.

Buildings are paid for with wood, clay, glass and brick. Everything else exists to feed the factory: food keeps your workers going, charcoal is the fire, water cools the glass, and wood ash is literally what makes old forest glass green.

Locked inBuildings cost wood, clay, glass, brick. The other four are fuel.
Point 04

You don't own a pile of stuff. You own two dials.

Here's the part that makes people stare. There is no pile of wood tokens in front of you. Instead, every good is a little marker sitting on a slice of a rotating wheel, and the slice it's sitting on tells you how many you have.

You get two wheels on your production board:

The Glassworks (top) holds sand, food, charcoal, water and wood, five goods in all, and produces glass.
The Brickworks (bottom) holds clay, food and charcoal, and produces brick.

Food and charcoal show up on both wheels, each with its own marker. So when a card gives you 2 food, you decide which wheel it lands on, but a single gain can't be split across both. That choice is a real decision, not bookkeeping.

Locked inTwo wheels: Glassworks (5 goods to glass), Brickworks (3 goods to brick).
Point 05

How the wheel actually makes glass

Each wheel is cut into 12 slices by two pointers. On the big side, 8 slices count your basic goods from 0 to 7. On the small side, 4 slices count your glass from 0 to 3.

Two of those slices are brown: the 0 slice on the big side and the 3 slice on the glass side. And here is the entire rule:

The moment both brown slices are empty, the wheel immediately clicks one slice clockwise. Both brown slices empty means: you have at least 1 of every single good on that wheel, and you're not already sitting on 3 glass.

One click = every basic good drops by 1, and glass goes up by 1. Five goods walk in, one glass walks out. Notice that the markers themselves never move; the pointer sweeps past them. Push the buttons and watch it happen.

Now the small one. Same machine, fewer parts: clay, food and charcoal, turning into bricks. It clicks far more often, which is why bricks feel cheap and glass feels precious.

Locked inAt least 1 of everything on a wheel = it fires, whether you like it or not.
Point 06

Three ways the wheel bites you

1. It's not optional. If the wheel can turn, it turns. You cannot hold back that last piece of wood to save it: the second you have one of everything, five goods are gone and you're holding glass instead.

2. There's a ceiling. Seven of any basic good per wheel, three glass, three bricks. Extra just evaporates. Gaining 4 wood when you already have 6 gets you exactly one useful wood.

3. It can chain. One gain can spin the wheel two or three times in a row. But a spin can never interrupt a payment: pay the full cost of a building first, then let the wheel sort itself out.

The sneaky trick hiding in rule 1: deliberately keeping one good at zero lets you stockpile everything else. Empty water bucket, full warehouse.

Locked inAutomatic, capped at 7 / 3, and it never interrupts a payment.
Point 07

Fifteen specialists, and everyone has the same fifteen

There is zero card luck in this game. You, and every opponent, hold an identical set of 15 specialist cards: Woodcutter, Charcoal Burner, Pond Builder, Fish Farmer, Feudal Lord, and so on.

At the start of every building period, you all secretly choose 5 of the 15 to take into your hand and put the other 10 aside face-down. At the start of the next period you get all 15 back and choose again from scratch.

Locked inSame 15 cards for everyone. Choose 5 secretly, each period.
Point 08

Every card is two workers in one

A specialist card carries two separate abilities, and sometimes an entry fee printed down the left edge. Pay the fee once, then use ability A, or ability B, or both, in whatever order you like. You just can't use the same one twice.

The Charcoal Burner is the clean example: entry fee 1 wood, and each ability gives 3 charcoal. Use both and you turned 1 wood into 6 charcoal.

Locked inTwo abilities per card. Entry fee paid once, no matter how many you use.
Point 09

The mind game: if someone copies you, you both get half

This is the heart of Glass Road. When you flip your card face-up, every opponent must check their hand. If they're holding the same card, they must play it right now, out of turn.

And the moment that happens, nobody gets both abilities. You each pick just one, and you can pick different ones. So:

Alone with the Charcoal Burner → 1 wood becomes 6 charcoal.
Someone matched you → 1 wood becomes 3 charcoal, and your opponent got 3 charcoal too, on your turn, for free.

Locked inAlone = both abilities. Matched = one each. Read the table.
Point 10

Three cards you lead, two you're betting on

You chose 5 cards but there are only 3 card rounds. Each round everyone picks a card, puts it face-down, then reveals in clockwise order starting from the player with the start player goblet.

So 3 of your 5 cards you play yourself. The other 2 only ever get used if someone else calls them out, and then you play them into the two slots on the edge of your board. Once both slots are full, you can't follow anyone anymore for the rest of the period. And while a slot is still open, you're not allowed to refuse.

That's the whole drafting puzzle: pick 3 cards you want, plus 2 cards you're confident someone else is going to play.

One easy detail to get wrong: if someone reveals the card you've already placed face-down for later, you do not flip it. It waits for its own round. Only cards still in your hand can follow.

Locked inLead 3, follow at most 2, and you can't decline while a slot is open.
Point 11

Your land is mostly in the way

Your landscape board starts covered: 6 forest tiles (each one covers two spaces), plus 2 pits, 2 groves, 2 ponds, and 3 buildings already printed on the left.

Buildings need empty spaces, so you have to clear ground. Pits, groves and ponds you may remove for free at any moment. Forests are different. Only certain cards shift them: the Woodcutter (2 or 4 wood), the Carpenter (fee: remove a forest), and the Slash-and-burn Farmer (2 charcoal and/or 2 food). Chopping trees pays.

But don't bulldoze everything. Several cards pay you per tile you own: the Pit Worker gives sand or clay for each pit, the Forest Manager food or wood per grove, the Pond Builder sand or water per pond. Land is both a floor and an income.

Locked inNo empty space, no buildings. Clearing forests needs specific cards.
Point 12

Three flavours of building

The central board holds three rows, and each row does a different job:

Processing (blue). A trade you may use any number of times, at any moment, including on someone else's turn. A Cooperage turns 1 wood into 2 water forever. These are your engine.
Immediate. A one-time payout the instant you build it.
Bonus. Silent all game, then scores at the end based on what you own.

You build with a card that allows it (the Cultivator, Builder, Carpenter or Supplier) by paying the cost in wood, clay, glass and brick. A tile with a wooden frame goes on an empty space. A tile with a brick pattern is an upgrade and stacks on top of a printed start building instead (and then only the upgrade scores).

The Feudal Lord is worth knowing early: it deals you 3 buildings that are yours alone, hidden from everyone else's grabbing hands.

Locked inBlue = reusable trade. Wooden frame = new space. Brick pattern = upgrade.
Point 13

How it ends, and how you win

After the fourth building period, stop and do the most satisfying thing in the game: everyone may use their processing buildings as many times as they want. Wring every last trade out of your engine, convert junk into whatever your bonus buildings are counting.

Then add up the value printed on your buildings, plus whatever your bonus buildings earn. Leftover goods are usually worth nothing, except through a bonus building like the Glassmaker's Colony, which pays half a point per quartz sand (and you don't round it, so 3 sand = 1.5 points).

Highest total wins. A tie means everyone tied wins. This is a friendly game about a nice forest.

Locked inMilk your processing buildings first, then only buildings score.
Point 14

Five things to do in your first game

Build in period 1. Even a cheap building. Twelve turns is nothing, and a building that helps you build is worth more than a perfect plan.

Look at your wheel before you spend. Ask "if I take this wood, does the wheel fire?" Sometimes that's exactly what you want. Sometimes it eats four goods you were saving.

For your 2 follow cards, take the obvious ones. Woodcutter, Charcoal Burner, Cultivator: the cards everybody wants. Getting a free half-card on someone else's turn is pure profit.

Clear two forests early. They pay you as you clear them and they open the space you'll desperately want in period 3.

Don't sit on 3 glass. A full glass slice jams the whole Glassworks. Spend it, then let the wheel keep turning.

Locked inYou know the game now. Go make some bottles.

Quick check

Your Glassworks shows sand 2, food 1, charcoal 1, water 2, wood 0 and glass 1. You gain 1 wood. What happens?

The wheel fires once. Every basic drops by 1 (sand 1, food 0, charcoal 0, water 1, wood 0) and glass goes to 2. It stops there, because food, charcoal and wood are now on the brown 0 slice.

You play the Fish Farmer. One opponent has it in hand. How many of its two abilities do you use?

One. So does your opponent, and they choose theirs independently of yours.

Three opponents each reveal a card you're holding a copy of, one after another. Do you play all three?

No, only the first two. You have exactly two slots per building period, and once they're full you can't follow anymore.

You finish the game with 4 glass, 6 wood and 11 points of buildings. What's your score?

11. (Also, you couldn't have had 4 glass; the wheel caps you at 3.) Goods only score through bonus buildings that specifically count them.
