Learn it properly · 14 points

Cooperative · one to fiveThe Lord of the Rings: Fate of the Fellowship

Matt Leacock took the bones of Pandemic and replaced the disease with despair. There is a war on the map and it is mostly a decoy. The game you are actually playing is about keeping two hobbits unobserved, and about one number that is your life total and your dice penalty at the same time.

1 The big picture

Everyone against the board, and only one way to lose

One to five players, all on the same side, a couple of hours. Each of you runs two characters out of a cast of thirteen, and Frodo and Sam are always in the game whoever else you pick.

You are trying to complete three story objectives and then destroy the Ring. Sauron is trying to do a great many things: burn the havens, march armies, and above all find Frodo. But all of that evil arrives in the same currency. Armies overrun a haven: you lose hope. The Eye settles on Frodo: you lose hope. A search goes badly: you lose hope.

If hope reaches zero, you have lost. That is the entire loss condition. Nothing else on the board kills you directly, which means every threat can be translated into the same question: how much hope is this going to cost me, and can I afford it?

The designer has said this out loud: an earlier version tracked the Ring corrupting Frodo, and it was replaced, because the struggle between hope and despair is the truer theme of the book. The change is why the game feels like it does. You can be battered and still recover.
Remember this

Everything bad in Middle-earth converts into one number. Learn to price threats in hope and the game becomes legible.

2 Your turn: four actions here, one action somewhere else

Two characters, and a deliberately unfair split of your attention

On your turn you take four actions with one of your characters and exactly one action with the other. Then you draw two player cards. Then the shadow deck answers you.

That four-and-one split is the cleverest small rule in the box. You have two people on a continent-sized map and you can only really be doing one thing. The second character is not a second worker, they are a reminder: somebody is moving one step per turn while the world burns somewhere else.

Actions are the ordinary verbs of a war: move along the roads, raise troops, march them, give battle, take a stronghold back, hand a card to someone standing next to you, complete an objective. Most of them are paid for with the icons on your cards.

Remember this

Four actions in one place and one action in another. The rule is telling you that you cannot be everywhere, so decide early where you are not going.

3 Your hand is a resource, a clock and a rescue kit at once

Every card you spend on the war is a card Frodo does not get

Player cards show regions, and along their edge, icons. The icons are what actions actually cost. Broadly: valour for fighting and taking strongholds back, friendship for raising troops and allies, stealth for hiding the hobbits, and the Ring for rerolling dice you cannot live with.

Three separate pressures live in that one hand of cards.

  • It is your economy. Every useful thing you do is paid for out of it.
  • It is the clock. The player deck is finite. If it cannot give you your two cards, you lose hope for every card it could not deal. The deck running dry is a real, calculable death.
  • It is Frodo's survival. Stealth icons are the only thing that lets the hobbits move safely, and Ring icons are the only thing that buys a second chance on a bad roll.

So the fundamental tension of the game is not "which threat do I answer first". It is this card, in my hand, right now: war or hobbits? A veteran group is recognisable by how much of their hand they refuse to spend.

Remember this

Cards are the whole economy and the hobbits are competing for them. Every stealth icon you burn on a battle is a search roll you will be making later.

4 The shadow deck, and its two faces

Evil arrives as either a march or a muster, and you can see which is coming

At the end of your turn you flip cards from the shadow deck. The backs come in two kinds, and the back tells you the flavour of the bad news before you read the card.

The backWhat happensWhat it means for you
MarchShadow armies advance along the battle lines toward your havens, and fight whatever they meetThe front moves. Undefended places change hands
MusterNew troops appear at a shadow location, and the Eye and the Nazgûl are repositioned, generally toward FrodoThe hunt tightens. The map gets heavier

How many you flip is the threat rate, and it climbs through the game. It never falls. The early game deals you one or two of these and feels manageable; the late game deals you a fistful every turn and feels like the walls closing in, which is precisely the intended shape of the story.

There is also the game's version of an epidemic: a card that darkens the skies, raises the threat rate, puts fresh troops out, and shuffles everything already discarded back on top of the deck, so the places that have already suffered are the places about to suffer again.

Remember this

The threat rate only rises. A plan that works this turn and takes four more turns to pay off may be a plan for a different, easier game.

5 Hope, the one number

Your life total, spent by every misfortune on the board

The hope track sits there and everything drains it. Havens falling costs you hope. The Eye finding Frodo costs you hope, two at a stroke. Search dice come up despair, you lose hope. The deck runs short, you lose hope.

And it goes back up, which is the part new players underrate. Taking a shadow stronghold back is worth two hope. Objectives restore hope. Characters and events restore hope. A group that has been beaten around the map for an hour can still be perfectly healthy, because hope is not damage, it is morale, and morale can be rebuilt.

This is why the war on the map is worth fighting even though Frodo is the win condition. The war is where hope comes from. You are not defending Gondor for Gondor's sake. You are defending it because losing it is a two-point hit and taking it back is a two-point gift, and those points are the fuel for the thing that actually wins.

Remember this

Hope is spendable and rebuildable. Treat it as a budget, not as a health bar you flinch at.

6 Frodo moves, and the world listens

Stealth is a cost, and if you do not pay it you roll for it

The hobbits walk east like anyone else, except for one rule: every time Frodo travels, you should be spending a stealth icon to go unseen. Pay it and the journey is quiet. Fail to pay it and you trigger a search.

A search is a handful of black dice, and the dice are counted from the situation you have walked into: one for every shadow troop and every Nazgûl in the region you entered, plus one for every point of hope you are missing. Seven is the ceiling. Each die can come up despair, which costs hope; other faces are survivable, and some send a Nazgûl back to Mordor, which is the closest thing the hobbits get to good news.

Read that middle term again, because it is the design's sharpest edge. Hope is not just your life total, it is your dice penalty. Lose some and every future search is more dangerous, which loses you more. The game has a downward spiral built into its core rule, and half of good play is refusing to enter it.

The instinct to unlearn

In most co-ops you spend your resources on the emergency in front of you and trust that you will find more. Here, spending your last stealth icon on a battle is how a quiet hobbit walk becomes six dice. Keep stealth in reserve the way you would keep a healing potion, not the way you keep small change.

Remember this

Pay the stealth or roll the dice. And the dice get worse precisely when you can least afford them.

7 The Eye

Sauron's attention is a token, and you can move it

The Eye of Sauron sits on a region of the map. It is not an army and it does not fight; it represents where the Dark Lord is looking. Shadow cards drag it toward Frodo, and when it arrives somewhere important the consequences are brutal: if the Eye is already in Frodo's region when Sauron's gaze next sweeps, you lose two hope on the spot.

The thing that makes this a great mechanism rather than just a threat is that you can pull the gaze away. Fighting attracts attention. A battle in the north, a stronghold stormed, a loud victory somewhere that does not matter: Sauron looks at the noise, and the hobbits are, for a moment, unregarded.

Once that clicks, the whole war on the map reads differently. Armies, sieges and heroes are not a second game bolted on to the hobbits' journey. They are the cover story. Half of what the Fellowship does is be interesting somewhere else, and Tolkien would recognise the strategy exactly.

Remember this

Attention is a resource you can spend. The war is loud on purpose.

8 The Nazgûl

Not an army: a set of dice that follow you around

The Nazgûl drift across the map toward wherever Sauron is looking, which is to say toward Frodo. They do not fight your armies in the ordinary way. What they do is add dice to searches, and that is a far nastier thing to be.

One Rider standing in the region you were about to walk into is one more die every time the hobbits move through. Three of them gathered on the road ahead and the road is effectively closed: you would be rolling a fistful of despair to take a single step.

They can be pushed back to Mordor, by lucky search results and by a few character abilities, and doing so is worth more than it looks, because you are not removing a monster, you are removing dice from every roll you make for the rest of the journey.

Remember this

Nazgûl are not blockers, they are a tax on every future step the hobbits take. Clear the road before you walk it.

9 Thirteen characters, and why the pair matters

You are not picking favourites, you are picking a division of labour

Each player takes two characters from the thirteen, and each has a special ability drawn straight from the story: a fighter who wins battles that ought to be lost, a captain who raises troops cheaply, a wanderer who covers ground, a wizard who mends things you thought were broken.

What makes the choice interesting is that the map has two jobs going at once, and every character leans toward one of them. Somebody has to walk beside the hobbits, feeding them stealth and taking the dangerous steps. Somebody has to hold the west together so the hope track does not bleed out. A team of five great warriors will win every battle and lose the game in Mordor.

The usual failure of a first game is emotional casting. You take the characters you love, they all turn out to be soldiers, and by the time you notice, the hobbits are alone at the edge of a very large map with nobody to hand them a card.
Remember this

Ask the table one question before anyone picks: who is going to Mordor, and who is holding the line?

10 Objectives: three acts before the ending

The story is shuffled, but its shape is not

Before Mount Doom you must complete three objectives, drawn from a deck of roughly two dozen. They are episodes from the book: break Isengard, hold a siege, rouse an ally, rescue someone who ought not to be where they are.

Because they are dealt fresh each time, no two games send you to the same places, which is where the replay value sits. But they always perform the same structural job: they are act breaks. Each one hands you a target, a scramble to hit it, and a reward, usually including hope, which lifts you just before the next wave of darkness pushes you back down.

Practically, they are also the best pacing tool you have. A group that completes its first objective early is a group with hope in the bank, working on the second while the threat rate is still low. A group that spends the first hour reacting to whatever the shadow deck did last has three objectives still to do in a world that is now twice as dangerous.

Remember this

Objectives are not side quests, they are the schedule. Start the first one on turn one, not when the map calms down, because it will not.

11 Mount Doom

The ending is a dice roll, and you spend the whole game buying it down

The final objective never changes. Frodo and Sam must reach Mount Doom with five Ring icons gathered up, and then make one last search roll. Survive it with at least a point of hope and the Ring goes into the fire.

The dice on that last roll are counted the way every search is counted, which means the hope you have left is the difficulty of the ending. Arrive with the track nearly full and you are rolling a couple of dice and will probably be fine. Arrive scraped down to your last point and you are rolling near the maximum of seven, needing to come through with something still standing.

What it looks like

Two groups both get the hobbits to the mountain on the same turn with the same five icons.

The first spent the game taking strongholds back and finishing objectives early; they roll a small handful of dice and win comfortably.

The second let three havens fall to save a turn here and there. They roll nearly seven and lose the game at the very last moment, which is the most Tolkien way to lose imaginable.

So hope is not something you spend down to zero as cleverly as possible. It is the currency you carry to the ending. Every point you rebuild in Rohan is a point of margin on the final roll.

Remember this

You are not racing to Mount Doom, you are arriving there rich. The ending is priced in hope.

12 The two clocks you are actually playing against

The deck runs out, and the threat rate runs up

Nothing in this game gives you unlimited time, and the limits are not dramatic, which is why they sneak up on people. There are two.

  • The player deck. Two cards a turn, forever, out of a finite stack. When it cannot pay you, you bleed hope. This is a hard ceiling on how many turns the game contains and you can literally count it.
  • The threat rate. Every darkening of the skies means more shadow cards per turn, permanently. The same map you were comfortably managing becomes unmanageable without anything visibly changing about your position.

Both clocks punish the same behaviour: tidiness. Cleaning up a region that was not going to kill you, shuffling characters into pretty positions, taking the safe turn again. The game is not scored on how neat the map looks. Nothing you do to the map matters except as it feeds the three objectives, the hope track and the road east.

Remember this

Turns are finite and each one is worse than the last. A turn that did not advance an objective, restore hope or move the hobbits was probably a wasted turn.

13 What good play actually looks like

Deciding what to lose, on purpose, early

Most of the skill in this game is triage, and triage means choosing the thing you are going to let burn. Some havens are worth two hope and a couple of turns to save. Some are worth two hope and nothing else, and the two turns you would spend are worth more than the two hope.

A good group talks in these terms out loud, early, and keeps a few things straight:

The habitWhy it works
Name the sacrificeAgreeing to abandon a corner of the map stops five players half-defending it for an hour
Escort the hobbitsSomeone should be near Frodo with cards in hand; stealth icons in the wrong hand are useless
Fight loudly, elsewhereBattles pull the Eye. Pick the battle whose noise helps the road east
Bank hope before you need itHope restored early lowers the difficulty of every search for the rest of the game
Count the deckKnowing you have eleven turns left changes which plans are real

Notice that none of these are clever combinations. This is a game about allocation under pressure, and the cleverness lives in what you refuse to do.

Remember this

You cannot save Middle-earth. You can save enough of it, for long enough, for two hobbits to walk out of the story.

14 How not to lose your first game

The five mistakes that make the last roll unwinnable

  • Spending stealth icons on the war. They look like ordinary currency and they are not. When Frodo needs to move and nobody has one, the spiral starts.
  • Defending everything. Every haven you rush to hold is a turn not spent on an objective, and the threat rate is rising the whole time. Pick what you are giving up.
  • Treating hope as a health bar. It is the difficulty dial of the ending. Rebuilding it in the quiet middle of the game is not housekeeping, it is the single highest-value thing you can do.
  • Leaving the hobbits unescorted. Cards cannot teleport. Somebody has to be standing next to Frodo to hand him what he needs, and that person has to have set off for Mordor long before it felt urgent.
  • Starting the objectives late. Three acts and a finale do not fit into the last third of a game that gets harder every turn. Begin the first one immediately, even clumsily.

And the single habit worth building: before each of your turns, ask which of the three things your turn is for. Advance an objective, restore hope, or move the hobbits east. If the answer is none of those, look again at the board, because something on it is quietly winning.

Remember this

The Ring is destroyed by a roll you can make easy or hard. Everything you do for two hours is an argument about how many dice you will be holding at the end.

That is Fate of the Fellowship. Hold the west, be loud in the wrong places, keep the two small ones unobserved, and arrive at the mountain with hope still in the bank.
