Fowers Games · 2014 · Word + Deck

Paperback

A crash course for new authors

You're a struggling pulp novelist. Spell words, get paid, buy better letters, and become famous before anyone else does.

Ten points · read in order
No.
01
The pitch

It's Scrabble and Dominion in a trench coat.

You're helping a writer named Paige Turner crank out cheap paperback novels: westerns, sci-fi, romance, crime. Every turn you spell a word out of the cards in your hand. Better words earn more money. Money buys better cards. Better cards let you spell better words.

But money isn't how you win. You win by collecting Fame points, printed on the beautiful fake book covers you buy. Most Fame at the end wins, and everything else in the game is just the machine you build to get there.

In plain words

Spell words → earn cash → buy cards → spell better words → buy book covers → win. Your vocabulary matters, but your shopping matters more.

No.
02
Your deck

You start with ten cards and not a single vowel.

Everyone begins with the exact same deck: the letters R, S, T, L, N plus five wild cards. Shuffle it, draw five, and you're an author.

RSTLN ?????

Wilds can be any letter you want, including vowels, which is lucky, because you own zero of those. The catch: a wild pays you 0¢. It fills a slot in your word and hands you nothing.

So a hand of five wilds can spell almost anything and earns you almost nothing. That trade-off is the entire game in one sentence.

In plain words

Wilds are flexible but broke. Letters are stiff but pay. You need both, and you'll spend the whole game arguing with yourself about the ratio.

No.
03
Your turn

Step one: put five cards down as a word.

Lay the cards you're using face-up in front of you, left to right, spelling a real word. A few ground rules:

  • You don't have to use all five cards. A short, expensive word can beat a long, cheap one.
  • No proper nouns: no names, no places, no brands.
  • Cards you don't use earn you nothing and get discarded anyway.

This is the part that scares people off, and it shouldn't. Unlike Scrabble, you're never staring at a board full of blocked squares. You have five cards, wilds to cover the gaps, and a shared vowel sitting in the middle of the table (that's next).

In plain words

Plurals and endings are your best friends. -S, -ED, -ING, -ER turn a boring 4-letter word into a paying 6-letter one.

No.
04
Shared letter

The Common card is a free letter for everybody.

In the middle of the table, one card is face up: the Common card, usually a vowel. On your turn you may use it in your word for free, once. You don't pick it up; you just point at it and say you're using it. It stays put for the next player.

So really you have six letters to work with, not five. New players constantly forget this and spell four-letter words all game.

In plain words

Look at the Common card first, then build around it. It's free money you already own.

No.
05
Payday

Only the letters you actually used pay you.

Every card has a small ¢ value printed on it. Add up the cents on the cards in your word, plus the Common card if you used it, and that total is your money for this turn.

Cards left in your hand are worth nothing. Wilds in your word are worth nothing. Rarer, pricier letters are worth more, which is why your starting R-S-T-L-N deck feels so poor so fast.

Any money you don't spend this turn vanishes. There's no saving up between turns, ever.

In plain words

A 7-letter word made of four wilds might pay less than a 3-letter word made of good letters. Count the cents, not the letters.

No.
06
Shopping

Spend it all, on as much as it'll cover.

In the middle of the table sits the Offer: piles of letter cards sorted by price, from cheap 2¢ letters up to the expensive powerhouses, plus the Fame cards. Spend your money on anything you can afford, and unlike most deck-builders, you can buy more than one card as long as the total fits your budget.

Everything you buy goes straight into your discard pile, not your hand. Then your whole hand and your purchases go to the discard, and you draw five fresh cards. When your deck runs out, shuffle the discard pile into a new deck, new cards included.

In plain words

Eight cents buys one great letter or two decent ones. Two decent ones thicken your deck; one great one sharpens it. Sharper usually wins.

No.
07
The trap

Fame cards win the game and wreck your deck.

Fame cards are the gorgeous fake novel covers, sold in four piles from cheap ones worth a few points up to prestige covers worth a small fortune. They're the only real way to score.

Here's the cruelty: a Fame card in your hand is just another wild worth 0¢. Every one you buy makes your future hands a little poorer. Buy too many too early and you'll be spelling beautiful words for two cents each while everyone else buys the good letters.

Buy none, though, and you lose with the sharpest deck at the table. Letters early, Fame late is the classic rhythm, and knowing exactly when to flip is the skill of the whole game.

In plain words

Fame cards are dessert. Eat them too early and you can't finish dinner. Skip them entirely and you starve.

No.
08
The long shot

Spell a monster word, keep the Common card.

The Common cards sit on a length track. If your word is at least as long as the number showing, you keep that Common card permanently: it's worth 5 Fame and becomes a useful vowel in your deck. Then the next Common card is revealed and the bar goes up:

78910Game end

Seven letters out of a five-card hand sounds impossible, which is why double-letter cards exist: some cards show two letters at once, like ED or NG. They must be used together, in that order, and they count as two letters toward your length.

In plain words

Two double-letter cards plus wilds plus the Common card gets you to eight letters fast. Free Fame, no money spent.

No.
09
Powers

Letters have abilities, but only if you use them.

Most bought letters carry a special power printed on them, and it fires only when that card appears in your word. Sitting unused in your hand, it does nothing.

  • Double letter score: the Scrabble classic, on a card you own forever.
  • Draw extra cards: a bigger hand next turn means a longer word.
  • Bonus cents: flat extra money, sometimes for each wild in your word.
  • Trash a card: the quiet superstar. It deletes a card from your deck permanently.

Trashing is how good players win. Every junk starting card you throw away means your good cards come around faster. Get rid of a couple of those 0¢ starting wilds early and your whole deck gets richer.

There are also attack cards that mess with the next player's hand. The rulebook suggests leaving those out of your very first game.

In plain words

Building a deck is subtraction as much as addition. A thin deck of great cards beats a fat deck with a few great cards in it.

No.
10
Last page

How it ends, and how to be the one holding the trophy.

The game finishes the moment two Fame piles run empty or the last Common card is claimed. Whoever triggered it finishes their turn, then everyone counts the Fame points across their entire deck: hand, draw pile, discard, all of it. Most Fame is the bestselling author.

Five things to remember on your first play:

  • Buy vowels early. You start with none, and they unlock every word you want to spell.
  • Trash your junk before you start buying Fame.
  • Watch the endgame clock. When Fame piles get low, stop improving your deck and start cashing out: a beautiful deck is worth zero points.
  • Chase the length track when you can. Common cards are Fame you didn't have to pay for.
  • Don't panic about your vocabulary. Wilds cover the letters you're missing, and nobody's judging your spelling. Probably.
The one-sentence version

Spend the early game making your hand richer, spend the late game turning that money into book covers, and never leave a cent unspent.
