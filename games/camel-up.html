Learn it properly · 14 points · second edition

Five camels, one pyramid of dice, and nobody owns a camelCamel Up

It looks like a racing game, and the camels are what everyone watches. But you never ride one. You are in the stands with a fistful of betting slips, and the whole game is deciding how much you trust a race that a pyramid full of dice is running.

1 The big picture

You are not a camel. You are a gambler at the track.

Camel Up is for three to eight people and takes about forty-five minutes. Five racing camels run once around a desert track. None of them belongs to anyone. Everybody at the table is a spectator, and the only thing you own is money, a small pile of Egyptian pounds that grows or shrinks depending on how well you bet.

At the end, the richest player wins. The camel that crossed the line first is irrelevant to you unless you put money on it.

That framing changes how you should watch every turn. A camel pulling ahead is not good news or bad news in itself. It is good news for whoever holds slips on that camel, and your job is to be holding the right slips at the moment the money is paid out.

Remember this

Cheer for your bets, not for a colour. The race is just the thing your money is riding on.

2 Nobody steers

The camels move by dice, and nobody chooses which die comes out.

There is no "move the red camel two spaces" decision in this game. Camels move only when somebody rolls, and a roll means shaking the pyramid, a plastic dice tower that holds one die per camel, and letting exactly one die drop out. Whatever colour falls out, that camel moves; whatever number it shows, one to three, is how far.

So the race itself is random, deliberately and completely. What is not random is what you know. You can see every camel's position, you can see which dice have already come out this round, and that is enough to reason about who is likely to be in front a few rolls from now.

This is the honest shape of the game: you cannot control the race, so you control your exposure to it. Every decision in Camel Up is a decision about which bet to make and when.
Remember this

You have no influence over which camel moves. Your skill is in reading the race, not running it.

3 The legs

Each die comes out once per leg, and that is where the information lives.

The race is cut into short rounds called legs. At the start of a leg, every die is back inside the pyramid. Each roll takes one die out and leaves it out. When all but one of the dice have come out, the leg ends, bets on that leg are paid, and everything goes back in for the next one.

Read that again, because it is the most important rule for the gambler in you: within a leg, a camel moves at most once. If the blue die has already been rolled this leg, blue is not moving again until the next leg, no matter how many more rolls happen. Its position is locked.

That means the race gets more predictable as a leg goes on. At the start, any camel could jump forward. Near the end, only two or three dice are left in the pyramid, and you can very nearly count out the possible finishes in your head.

Remember this

Watch which dice are already out. A camel whose die has been rolled is frozen until the next leg.

4 Riding on top

Camels stack, and the one on top is carried for free.

This is the rule that makes Camel Up wild. When a camel lands on a space that already has camels on it, it does not share the space politely. It climbs on top of them. And when a camel moves, it carries everything stacked above it along for the ride.

Two consequences follow, and they run against every racing instinct you have:

  • On the same space, the camel on top is ahead. Of a stack, the top camel counts as the leader and the bottom one as the last.
  • A camel at the bottom of a stack is a delivery service. When it rolls, the whole tower moves, and the camel sitting on top arrives in front without its own die ever coming out.

Stacks are why the race lurches. A camel that looks hopelessly behind can jump on top of the pack with one roll and be leading, then be carried two more spaces by somebody else's die before its own comes out.

Remember this

Position is not just which space; it is how high up. A camel on top of a stack has every die beneath it working for it.

5 One thing per turn

On your turn you do exactly one thing, and three of the four are ways to bet.

Turns are fast. You choose one of four actions and then it is the next player's go:

  • Take a leg bet: grab a betting tile for a camel you think will be in front when this leg ends.
  • Place your spectator tile on the track, so you earn a coin whenever a camel lands on it.
  • Bet on the whole race: secretly back a camel to win, or to come last, overall.
  • Roll the pyramid: move a camel, and take a ticket that pays one coin.

Notice what is missing: there is no way to spend money, no shop, no building. Coins only come in through bets paying out, the spectator tile, and rolling. Every one of these is a small wager of a single turn, and turns are the thing you are actually short of.

The next several points take these actions one at a time, because each one is good at a different moment in a leg.

Remember this

Each turn is one decision about risk. The question is never "what can I do?" but "which of these four is worth most right now?"

6 Leg bets

The first slip on a camel pays the most. That is the race within the race.

For each camel there is a small stack of leg betting tiles, and they shrink as they are taken. The first player to back a camel this leg takes a tile worth 5; the next takes a 3; after that only 2s remain. When the leg ends, each tile is settled:

Your camel at the end of the legWhat the tile does
In first placepays its printed value (5, 3 or 2)
In second placepays 1
Anywhere elsecosts you 1

So there is a tension built into every leg. Bet early and you get the fat 5 tile, but the race is wide open and you are guessing. Wait for a few dice to come out and you can bet almost safely, but somebody else will already have taken the good tile and you are left with a 2.

The best leg bets sit in the middle: the moment the race has given away enough information to make one camel a clear favourite, but before everybody else has noticed. Spotting that moment a turn sooner than the player to your left is most of what a good Camel Up player does.

The instinct to unlearn

"Wait until I'm sure" feels prudent. In this game, by the time you are sure, so is everyone else, and the tile is gone. A 5 at decent odds beats a 2 at certainty.

Remember this

The tile shrinks while the odds improve. Bet at the moment the camel becomes a favourite, not the moment it becomes a sure thing.

7 A bet is a price

Do the sum once, roughly, and most turns become obvious.

You do not need to be good at probability to play Camel Up. You do need one habit: before you take a betting tile, give the camel a rough chance of winning the leg and see what the tile is worth at that chance. Compare it with rolling, which always pays exactly 1.

What it looks like at the table

Red is sitting on top of a stack, one space ahead. You reckon red wins this leg half the time, comes second three times in ten, and is worse than second the other two times in ten.

The 5 tile: half of 5 is 2.5, plus 0.3 for second places, minus 0.2 for the misses. About 2.6 coins.

The 2 tile on the same camel: 1.0 plus 0.3 minus 0.2. About 1.1 coins.

Rolling is worth 1. So the 5 tile is a great turn, the 2 tile is barely better than a roll, and a 2 tile on a weaker camel is worse than rolling.

That last line is the lesson. Late in a leg, when only 2s are left, rolling the pyramid and taking the sure coin is often the better action, even though it feels like doing nothing.

Remember this

A roll is a guaranteed 1. A bet has to beat that on average, and the small tiles on unlikely camels never do.

8 Rolling is a move too

Rolling pays a coin, but it also spends everyone's information.

Rolling looks like the passive action, the one you take when nothing better is on offer. It is more pointed than that. Every roll takes a die out of the pyramid, which makes the rest of the leg easier to predict, and the next player gets to bet on that clearer picture before you do.

So a roll is a gift to whoever goes after you, unless the roll lands in your favour. Good players roll for one of two reasons: either no bet is worth more than the coin, or they are holding slips and want to push the leg towards its end while their camel is in front.

The last roll of a leg is the purest version. If your camel leads and only one die is left to come out of the pyramid before scoring, rolling ends the leg and locks your payout in. Somebody else rolling might be the die that ruins it.

Rolling is also the only action that is always available. If every tile on the favourite has gone and the spectator tile is already out, the pyramid is still there, still paying one coin.
Remember this

Before you roll, ask who benefits from the clearer picture. If it is the next player, find something else to do.

9 Your spectator tile

A tile on the sand that pays you rent and nudges the race.

Each player has one spectator tile, a little crowd in the desert. You place it on an empty stretch of track, cheering side up or booing side up. Every time a camel lands on it, you collect a coin, and the camel is pushed one extra space forward (cheering) or one space back (booing).

That makes the tile two things at once. It is an income: put it where the pack is likely to land in the next few rolls and it can pay out more than once in a leg. And it is a lever: a cheering tile just in front of your camel gives it an extra step, and a booing tile in front of a rival's camel drags it back.

A camel pushed back by a booing tile slides in underneath any camels already on the space behind, rather than on top. A small detail with a big effect, because the bottom of a stack is last place on that space.

Remember this

Put your tile where camels will land, and on the side that helps your slips. The best spectator tile is income and sabotage in the same turn.

10 The crazy camels

Two more camels run the course backwards, carrying anyone foolish enough to sit on them.

The second edition adds two crazy camels, one black and one white, that nobody bets on and that run the track in the wrong direction. They share a single grey die in the pyramid. When it comes out, its face says which of the two moves, and that camel walks backwards.

Crazy camels obey the stacking rule like everyone else, and that is the whole point of them. If racing camels are sitting on top of a crazy camel when it moves, they get carried backwards with it. A favourite that climbed onto the wrong stack can lose two or three spaces on a die that had nothing to do with its colour.

They also put a crack in the reasoning from point 3. You can count out the five racing dice, but as long as the grey die is still in the pyramid, any stack with a crazy camel in it is a stack that might suddenly run the wrong way.

Remember this

Check what your favourite is standing on. A camel riding a crazy camel is one grey die away from going the wrong way.

11 The long bets

Back the overall winner, or the overall loser, early and in secret.

Besides the leg bets, you hold a hand of cards, one per camel colour. Spending a turn, you can place one face down on the overall winner pile or the overall loser pile. Nobody knows which camel you backed until the race ends.

The piles pay out in the order cards were played. The first correct card on the winner pile collects 8, the next correct one 5, then 3, then less and less. Every wrong card costs a coin. The loser pile works the same way for the camel that finishes last.

That ordering is the same tension as leg bets, stretched across the whole game. A long bet placed in the first leg is a long shot but pays 8 if you are right. A long bet placed when one camel is three spaces from the finish is nearly safe, but the 8 and the 5 are probably already in somebody else's card.

The loser bet is the one new players forget. Picking a winner means guessing the top of a lurching race; picking a loser is often easier, because one camel stuck at the back with nothing to ride on tends to stay there.

The instinct to unlearn

A hidden card feels like information you are keeping safe. It is also information you are giving away by timing: when an experienced player suddenly makes a long bet, everyone else starts looking at which camel just took the lead.

Remember this

Long bets reward courage, and the loser pile is half the market. Commit to at least one long bet before the race looks settled.

12 Scoring and the finish

Money changes hands at the end of every leg, and once more at the line.

Scoring in Camel Up is not saved up for the end. At the end of every leg, the leg bets are settled, the pyramid tickets each pay a coin, and the tiles go back for the next leg. Your money goes up and down all game, in full view.

The race ends the moment any camel crosses the finish line, even in the middle of a leg. That leg is scored as normal, and then the two long-bet piles are turned over and paid out in order. Then you count coins, and the richest player wins.

Two things worth knowing before your first finish:

  • The finish can come suddenly. A stack ridden by the right camel can jump several spaces in one roll. If the leaders are close to the line, every roll might be the last.
  • The long-bet piles are often the biggest swing of the night. A player who looked behind all game can turn up an 8 and a 5 and take the lead in the last minute.
Remember this

The race ends without warning. When camels are near the line, the last leg bet you place may be your last chance to bet at all.

13 The table is the game

It is loud, it is quick, and the other players are your real competition.

Every bet you can make is also a bet somebody else can take first. There is only one 5 tile per camel each leg, and only one 8 on the winner pile. So the player who hurts you most is rarely the dice; it is the player one seat ahead of you, who saw the same favourite you did and took its tile.

This is also why Camel Up plays so well at a crowd. With more people, each leg sees fewer turns per player, the good tiles vanish quicker, and timing gets sharper. At the largest player counts the second edition lets players pair up for a leg and share a bet, which is as much a social move as a mathematical one.

Camel Up won the Spiel des Jahres in its first edition for exactly this reason: it is a game where the most fun moment is groaning at a die together, and nobody needs to understand much to share it.
Remember this

Watch the bets, not just the camels. What other players have backed tells you which tiles are gone and which ones they will grab next.

14 How not to lose your first game

Five instincts that other games gave you, and all five cost money here.

You picked a favourite colour

It is natural to adopt a camel and back it all game. The camels do not care. Each leg is a fresh market, and the best bet this leg is often on a camel you bet against last leg.

You waited until you were sure

Certainty arrives about a turn after the 5 tile is gone. Take the big tile when a camel becomes the likely leader, not when it is the only possible one.

You took every small bet going

A 2 tile on a camel with a modest chance of winning loses money on average. If no bet beats the sure coin from rolling, roll.

You forgot the stacks

Counting spaces is not enough. A camel two spaces behind but sitting at the bottom of a tall stack, or one in front but standing on a crazy camel, is not where it looks.

You never made a long bet

The winner and loser piles hold the biggest single payouts in the game, and they reward going in early. A first-time player who skips them is giving the rest of the table the 8s.

The whole game in one line

Nobody steers, so everybody bets. Read which dice are left and what each camel is standing on, take the big tile one turn before everyone else, and roll when no bet beats a sure coin.
