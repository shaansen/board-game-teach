Learn it properly · 14 points

Fourteen species, ten kinds of planet, six rounds and no warGaia Project

Gaia Project looks like a space game and plays like a logistics problem. You are not conquering anything. You are trying to reach planets you can live on, before the person whose home world is two colours away from yours reaches them first, and to fund the research that makes reaching them cheap.

1 The big picture

You are a species that can breathe one atmosphere, in a galaxy of nine others.

One to four players each take one of fourteen species. Each lives on one of seven basic planet types, and your buildings can only stand on your own type. Any other planet has to be terraformed, which means changing its atmosphere step by step until it matches you.

The galaxy is built at setup out of hexagonal sectors, so the map is different every game, but the shape of the problem never is: the planets you can afford are scattered, and the planets near you are mostly the wrong colour.

The game runs six rounds. Each round you take income, then players alternate single actions until everyone has passed. Scores land somewhere around 130 to 160 points in a decent game, and they come from six things at once: the round scoring tiles, six research tracks, federations, tech tiles, two final scoring conditions, and your leftover resources.

There is no attacking. The interaction is exactly two things, the same two as in its ancestor Terra Mystica: the good planets run out, and your neighbours hand you power every time they build near you. Everything else is you against your own economy.

If you already know Terra Mystica, the structure will feel familiar within ten minutes. The genuinely new parts are range, the six research tracks, and the two final scoring tiles. Those three are what make it a different game rather than a reskin.
Remember this

Nobody can stop you doing anything. They can only get to the planet first, and make everything you do afterwards cost more.

2 The planet wheel

Every planet has a price for you, and a different price for the player opposite.

The seven basic planet types sit in a fixed circle. Terraforming moves a planet around that circle one step at a time, and each step costs ore. One step is cheap, three steps is a serious investment, and the distance is decided entirely by which species you happen to be.

Two planet types sit outside the wheel and cannot be terraformed at all. Gaia planets are green and anyone may settle one by paying a unit of the game's rare currency instead of terraforming. Transdim planets are purple and are not planets yet; they have to be converted first, which is point 9.

As in every game of this family, the wheel also tells you who your real rival is. The species one or two steps from you wants the same hexes you do. The species on the far side of the circle is playing a different game on the same board.

Remember this

Look at the galaxy through your own colour. Half the planets on the table are not really available to you, and knowing which half is most of your opening plan.

3 Range: the rule that has no equivalent anywhere else

You may only build within reach, and reach starts at almost nothing.

Terraforming a planet is not enough. You have to be able to get there. Every build measures the distance in hexes from your nearest existing building to the target planet, and that distance must be within your current navigation range, which starts at one hex.

One hex is very short. It means your opening position dictates your first several builds, and it means the galaxy is not an open board: it is a slowly expanding bubble around wherever you started.

Range is bought in two ways. The navigation research track raises it permanently, which is why that track feels less like science and more like infrastructure. Or you spend a Q.I.C., the game's scarce fourth currency, to stretch your range for a single build and no longer.

This changes the texture of the whole game compared to its ancestor. Space is not just contested, it is gated. A planet nobody else wants can be unreachable for you until round four, and a planet three players want may be reachable by only one of them right now. Before you covet a hex, measure it.

Remember this

Where you are allowed to build is a resource like ore or credits. Spending your whole game at range one is the quietest way to lose.

4 The shape of a round

Income, then one action at a time until everybody has stopped.

Each round runs the same four steps, and they take about as long to learn as to read.

  • Income. Everything your buildings, your booster and your tech tiles produce arrives at once. This is the only moment resources appear.
  • Gaia phase. Power you committed to gaia-forming comes back, and transdim planets you were working on finish turning green. Point 9.
  • Actions. Players alternate, one action each, until all have passed. Build, upgrade, research, form a federation, take a power action, take a special action.
  • Cleanup. The round scoring tile is discarded and the next one is exposed.

Passing does the same triple duty it does in every game of this family. It ends your round, it lets you swap your booster tile for one of the face-up alternatives, and the first player to pass goes first next round. There is exactly one more booster than there are players, so the last player to pass takes whatever is left.

The thing worth internalising is that resources are turns. A player who collects more income than you simply acts more times this round, and the actions they take after you have passed are the ones that take the planets you were saving.

Remember this

Everything arrives in income and nothing arrives during the round. Your income sheet is your turn count.

5 The buildings, and what each one is really for

Five structures, and every one of them is a different job.

Your buildings sit on your player board covering printed income. Building one takes it off the board and reveals what it pays, every round, from then on.

StructureHow you get itWhat it is for
MineTerraform and settle a planetOre, and a foothold to measure range from
Trading stationUpgrade a mineCredits and power; cheaper to build next to a rival
Research labUpgrade a trading stationKnowledge, and a tech tile
AcademyUpgrade a research labKnowledge or a repeatable special action, plus a tech tile
Planetary instituteUpgrade a trading stationYour species' signature ability

Three things deserve saying out loud. First, upgrading replaces income rather than adding to it: the mine goes back onto your board and stops paying ore. A tableau of trading stations is a player with credits and no ore to build with.

Second, a trading station costs less to build if the planet is next to another player. The game actively pays you to move in beside someone, and then charges you for the power they collect when you do.

Third, research labs and academies are how tech tiles enter your game, and tech tiles are how you climb the research tracks at any speed. That connection is the spine of the whole thing, and it is point 8.

Remember this

Mines make the ore that makes more mines. Build wide before you build tall, or round four arrives with nothing to spend.

6 Power cycles, it does not accumulate

Three bowls, one direction of travel, and a bill your neighbours pay in points.

Your power tokens live in three areas. Gaining power moves tokens one area to the right, from 1 to 2 to 3. Spending power takes tokens out of area 3 and returns them all the way to area 1. Nothing is ever created. It takes two power gained to make one power spendable.

Power pays for the shared power actions on the main board: a lump of knowledge, ore, credits, victory points, a terraforming step, a Q.I.C. Each is available once per round and goes to whoever reaches for it first. Power also runs the special abilities of several species and several tech tiles.

Then there is charging. When a player builds or upgrades next to your buildings, you are offered power equal to the power value of your adjacent structures, and you pay one victory point for every power taken beyond the first. Three power costs two points.

The decision that comes up twenty times a game

Round one: three power for two points. Take it. That power becomes a power action, and a power action in round one gets its value back several times over.

Round six: the same three power for the same two points, and you will pass before you can spend it. Decline.

Early power is cheap at the price. Late power is a donation.

There is a fourth area, the gaia area, which is where power goes when you commit it to gaia-forming. It comes back, but only after a round out of circulation, which is exactly what makes gaia-forming an investment rather than a move.

Remember this

Count area 3, not your whole supply. The rest is power you own and cannot use yet.

7 The six research tracks

This is the part that makes it Gaia Project. It is engine and scoreboard at the same time.

Six tracks run up the board, each five steps tall. You climb one step at a time by spending knowledge, and each step you pass pays you something as you go. At the end of the game, your position on some of them scores directly.

TrackWhat climbing it buysWho it is for
TerraformingEach step of atmosphere costs less oreAnyone planning to settle awkward planets, which is everyone
NavigationPermanent range, so more of the map exists for youCramped starts and sprawling plans
Artificial intelligenceQ.I.C., the currency that buys range, tech and planetsFlexible play, and the Gaia planet route
Gaia projectGaiaformers, and cheaper gaia-formingMaps with a lot of purple in reach
EconomyMore income of everything, every roundLong games and early investment
ScienceMore knowledge, which is more researchClimbing faster, in order to climb faster

Knowledge is the tightest resource in the game. It arrives slowly, mostly from research labs and academies, and there is no way to convert much of anything else into it. So the tracks are not a menu you browse, they are a small number of ladders you can actually afford to climb, chosen in the first two rounds.

The top step of each track is special: only one player may ever stand there, it costs a federation token to unlock, and it hands over the largest single effects in the game. That makes the tracks quietly competitive. If two players are climbing the same one, the second one arrives to find the door locked.

The spread that loses

Level one on five different tracks feels like progress and buys almost nothing. Level three or four on two tracks changes what your turns cost for the rest of the game. Depth beats breadth, and it is not close.

Remember this

Pick your two or three tracks in round one and commit. Every step is a permanent discount, and discounts compound over six rounds.

8 Tech tiles

Research is not something you buy. It is something you are handed, with a condition attached.

You cannot simply spend knowledge and walk up a track. To advance, you take a tech tile from the display above the tracks, and taking one both gives you its printed bonus and moves you one step up a track of your choice. Tech tiles come from building research labs, academies and your planetary institute, and from a few boosters and federations.

This is the join that makes the whole machine hang together, and it is the part new players see last:

  • Mines make ore, which builds more mines and upgrades them.
  • Upgrades make research labs, which make knowledge and hand you tech tiles.
  • Tech tiles move you up research tracks, which make terraforming cheaper and range longer.
  • Cheaper terraforming and longer range mean more mines, further out, on better planets.

Some tiles are advanced: they sit above the standard ones, they are worth considerably more, and taking one costs a federation token and covers up a standard tile you already own. That is the late-game upgrade path, and it is the second job federations do.

Remember this

Research labs are not a luxury. They are the valve the whole engine runs through, and a game with none of them is a game with no second half.

9 Gaia planets and the purple ones

A slow route to planets nobody can take from you.

Scattered through the galaxy are transdim planets, purple and uninhabitable. They are not on the wheel and no amount of terraforming touches them. To use one you send a gaiaformer, a piece you only get by climbing the gaia project track, and you commit power tokens into the gaia area to run it.

At the start of the next round the planet turns green: it becomes a Gaia planet, your gaiaformer comes home, and your power tokens come back into circulation. A Gaia planet can then be settled by paying a Q.I.C., without terraforming at all.

Two honest things about this route. It is slow: a planet takes a round of waiting and locks up power while it waits. And it is safe: nobody else can take a transdim planet without doing the same work, and several scoring tiles and species reward Gaia planets specifically.

So gaia-forming is a map-reading decision, not a taste. Look at what is in range of your start. If two or three purple planets sit within reach, the gaia track is a strategy. If there is one on the far side of the galaxy, it is a distraction with a nice colour.

Remember this

Gaia-forming buys planets with time instead of ore. Time is the resource you have least of in a six round game, so only pay it where the map is generous.

10 Federations

The biggest single payout in the game, and it needs planning three turns out.

Look at a connected group of your own buildings. Add up their power values: one for a mine, two for a trading station or research lab, three for an academy or planetary institute, four for the planetary institute of some species. When a connected group reaches seven power value, you may declare it a federation and take a federation token.

Connected means adjacent, and where your buildings are not adjacent you may link them with satellites, small markers dropped onto empty space, each paid for with a power token that leaves your economy for good. So a spread-out empire can still federate, it just pays for the privilege.

A federation token is worth a great deal. It pays victory points and a lump of resources immediately, and afterwards it is the key that unlocks two things nothing else unlocks: the top step of a research track and advanced tech tiles. Most winning games contain two or three federations. Most first games contain one, formed by accident in round five.

What planning one looks like

You have a mine, a mine and a trading station in one corner: 1 + 1 + 2 = 4. An academy upgrade on the trading station would make it 1 + 1 + 3 = 5, still short.

A fourth mine two hexes away, plus one satellite to bridge the gap, takes it to 6, and the upgrade you wanted anyway takes it to 7.

Three moves you had already planned, arranged in an order that also pays out a federation.

Remember this

Seven power value, connected. Count your clusters before every build, because the same buildings in a different order are worth a federation more.

11 The scoring tiles aim the whole game

Six round tiles tell you when. Two final tiles tell you what for.

Six round scoring tiles are laid out at setup, face up, one per round. Each says what earns bonus points that round: points per mine built, per trading station, per research step, per Gaia planet settled, per federation formed. They are worth twenty to thirty points over a game, and they are the only part of Gaia Project that tells you when to do the things you were going to do anyway.

Then there are two final scoring tiles, also face up from the start, and they are the ones new players walk straight past. Each ranks all players on some measure at the end of the game, for a large chunk of points: most buildings in a single sector, most planet types settled, most satellites, most buildings on Gaia planets. First place scores a lot, and every place scores something.

Those two tiles should be shaping decisions from your very first build. If the game will count planet types, then settling your sixth planet of the same colour is worth less than settling an awkward one you have never touched. If it counts buildings in one sector, then sprawling across four sectors is actively wrong.

The most expensive thing you can ignore

The two final tiles are usually worth more than any single research track. Players who read them in round five discover that the game they have been playing for five rounds scores badly in the game that is actually being scored.

Remember this

Read all eight tiles before your first action. The round tiles set your calendar and the final two set your destination.

12 Your species is the plan

Fourteen of them, and they disagree about what a good turn looks like.

Each species has its own home planet type, its own starting income and research positions, its own costs, and a planetary institute ability that is the reason to play it. A sample of the range:

The practical reading is the same as with the research tracks. Your species board is telling you which of the systems in this briefing is cheap for you, and the cheap system is the one you should be doing more of than anyone at the table. The planetary institute is usually the build that turns your species on, which is a strong argument for getting it up in round two or three rather than admiring it until round five.

Remember this

Nobody in Gaia Project does everything. Find the thing you do more cheaply than the table, and do it far more often than feels reasonable.

13 Round six, and the count

The last round is not played like a round. It is played like a liquidation.

When the sixth round ends, four things are counted:

  • The two final scoring tiles, ranking everyone on whatever they measure. Large.
  • The research tracks, which pay out for the heights you reached.
  • Federation tokens and tech tiles that carry end-game points.
  • Leftover resources, at a poor rate: every few credits is a single point, and ore and knowledge convert to credits first.

That last line is why the final round looks so frantic. Anything you are still holding is worth a fraction of what it is worth spent, so the correct round six converts every scrap of knowledge into research, every scrap of ore into buildings, and every spendable power into a power action. Ending the game with a full supply is handing back points you had already earned.

The other round six habit is arithmetic. The final scoring tiles rank players, and the difference between first and second on one of them is often larger than anything else you can do with your last two actions. Work out what those two tiles will say before you decide what your last turns are for, because a single cheap mine on a planet type you have never settled can be worth more than a research step.

Remember this

In round six, stop building an engine and start cashing it. Count the two final tiles first, then spend down to nothing.

14 How not to lose your first game

The five habits that cost beginners the most.

Gaia Project will never tell you that you are losing. There is no attack, no bankruptcy, no disaster. You simply arrive at round four with four credits, one ore, nowhere in range and nothing to do. These are the reasons.

You spread your research thin

One step on four tracks is four small bonuses. Three steps on terraforming is every planet you ever settle costing a third less. Choose two tracks in round one, and keep choosing them.

You ignored range

Range one is a cage, and the map does not open up on its own. If you have not climbed navigation and have no Q.I.C. saved, the good planets in round four are simply not addressable by you, whatever you can afford.

You built tall before you built wide

Mines are the ore, and ore is everything else. Upgrading your only two mines into trading stations in round two produces a player with money, no ore and nothing to do with either. Get four or five mines down first.

You declined power because it cost points

Two points for three power in round one is one of the best trades on the table, and beginners refuse it because the score is right there on the board. Take it early, decline it in round six. The only question is whether you will spend the power before the game ends.

You did not read the two final tiles

They are face up from the very start and they are worth more than most research tracks. Playing a beautiful sprawling game when the tile rewards a single dense sector is the most common way a good-looking first game finishes last.

The whole game in one line

Six rounds, one action at a time, on planets you can only settle if you can both afford them and reach them. Build wide, dig deep into two research tracks, take the power, count to seven, and play towards the two tiles that were face up all along.
