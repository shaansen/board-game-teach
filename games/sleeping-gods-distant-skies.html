Learn the whole game · 14 points

Sleeping Gods

Distant Skies

A cargo plane, a portal that closed behind you, and a spiral-bound world that only ever gets smaller. You are not playing against a person. You are playing against a book that remembers everything you did.

1 The big picture

A cooperative campaign where the opponent is a storybook.

One to four of you crew a cargo plane that flew through a hole in the sky over San Francisco in 1937 and came out somewhere else. That somewhere is an atlas: a spiral-bound book of illustrated map pages, one enormous continuous world, and your plane sits on it as a single marker.

Nobody controls the world. A second book, the storybook, does. Every interesting place on the map has a number; you go there, you look the number up, you read the passage aloud, and the passage offers you choices. That is the entire loop of the game, and everything else on the table exists to decide whether your choices are allowed to work.

This is a campaign, not an evening. You play a session, write down where the plane is and what state the crew is in, put the box away, and come back to the same world next week. A full campaign is typically several sessions long.

The goal is to get home. Doing that means finding and restoring dormant totems scattered across the world, which wakes the gods who can open the way back. But there are far more totems in the atlas than you need, and far more world than you will see. You are not trying to finish the map. You are trying to finish before your time runs out.

Remember this

You are three books and a plane: an atlas that says where, a storybook that says what happens, and a deck of cards that says whether you are good enough. The cards exist to buy you passages in the book.

2 The atlas

Everywhere you look is spent forever.

The atlas pages tile into one map. Your plane marker moves across it, and dotted onto it are numbered spots: villages, ruins, coastlines, strange lights on a ridge. When you spend an action to Explore one, you read the matching storybook passage.

Then you cross the number off. That location is done for the rest of the campaign, whether the passage gave you a totem, a quest, a friend, a mauling, or two lines of scenery and nothing else.

This is the quiet cruelty of the design and the thing that makes it a game rather than a novel. Exploration is not free browsing. Each number is a card you get to turn over once, and turning over the wrong ones is how campaigns are lost.

The trap

Sweeping the map. Beginners explore every number they fly over "to see what's there", burn a quarter of the campaign clock on scenery, and arrive at the endgame with two totems and no fuel. Curiosity is the expensive resource here, not fuel.

Remember this

Every number on the atlas is one-use. Explore on purpose, with a reason you could say out loud.

3 Your turn

Five ticks of a pocket watch, and everything costs one or two.

A turn is small and completely legible. You draw ability cards up to your hand limit, you resolve an event, and then you have five time on the watch track to spend. When it runs out, your turn ends and the next player starts theirs.

ActionTimeWhat it does
Travel1–2Move on foot across the map; rough terrain costs the extra
Fly2Move the plane a longer hop, burning fuel and inviting damage
Explore2Read the numbered passage where you are standing
Repair1Patch health back onto the plane
Camp—Free in time, but crosses a camp icon off your journey log

There is one lovely wrinkle. If you stop with one tick left rather than spending it, the next player gets a bonus time token: an extra tick on their turn. A tick you cannot use well is worth more handed sideways than dribbled away on half a move.

Remember this

Five time is two real actions and a bit. Plan the turn as a pair of decisions, not as five small ones.

4 The crew

Five people, six skills, and two things that run out.

There are five characters. In a full game each player runs their own; solo you run two; and whoever is taking the current turn also has Claire, the captain, on loan. Each has a board with a portrait, a set of skill numbers, and two tracks that matter constantly.

  • Health is how much punishment the character can take. At zero they are incapacitated: out of challenges and combat until somebody spends time restoring them.
  • Stamina, up to three tokens, is how often they can be useful. Joining a challenge costs a stamina. Many equipped abilities cost a stamina too.

The six skills are Strength, Cunning, Savvy, Perception, Craft and Moxie, and each character is strong in two or three of them and hopeless in the rest. The storybook constantly asks for one specific skill, so the crew is a keyring: the question is never "are we good enough", it is "did we bring the right person and does she have a stamina left".

The trap

Treating stamina as a health bar and hoarding it. Stamina comes back every time you camp; unspent stamina at the campfire is a challenge you declined to attempt for free.

Remember this

Health is whether a character exists. Stamina is whether they can help today. They are refilled by the same thing, and it is not free.

5 Ability cards

One deck that is your hand, your dice and your equipment at the same time.

Distant Skies has no dice. It has a single deck of ability cards, and that deck does three unrelated jobs, which is the cleverest thing in the box.

  • Randomiser. Every card has a fate number printed on it. Turning the top card over is how the game rolls.
  • Fuel. Cards in hand can be discarded during a challenge for +1 each, provided they match the skill being tested.
  • Gear. Cards can instead be equipped to a character, paying their cost in other cards from your hand. Equipped, they add a permanent skill bonus and usually a special ability.

So every card in your hand is a small dilemma. Spend it now to squeak past a target number, or pay it into a piece of equipment that makes every future check of that skill easier. A hand of seven is not seven resources; it is roughly two equipment purchases or seven one-point nudges.

Remember this

Cards spent on a challenge are gone the moment the passage is read. Cards spent on equipment work for the rest of the campaign.

6 The challenge

Skill plus fate plus whatever you are willing to burn.

The storybook says something like Perception 9. Here is the whole procedure, and it does not get more complicated than this all campaign:

  • Decide which characters are joining. Each one who does spends a stamina.
  • Add up their skill in the tested skill, plus bonuses from anything they have equipped.
  • Draw fate: flip the top ability card and add its fate number.
  • Now, knowing the total, discard matching cards from hand for +1 each, and fire off any abilities you want to pay for.
  • Meet or beat the target and you get the good branch. Fall short and the passage tells you exactly what it costs you.

Note the order. Because the fate draw happens before you commit cards, you are never guessing. You are looking at an exact shortfall and deciding whether this particular passage is worth two cards out of your hand.

Remember this

You always know the price of success before you pay it. The skill is deciding when the answer is no.

7 Failure

Missing a check is a plot point, not a dead end.

Come up short and the storybook simply reads you the other paragraph. Sometimes that is damage to a character, sometimes a status effect, sometimes losing a resource, and quite often it is just a worse version of the same story that still moves you forward.

This matters for how you should feel at the table. In most cooperative games a failed roll is a punch in the face. Here it is usually a toll. A campaign in which you never fail a check is a campaign in which you were too cautious to be anywhere interesting.

The real cost of a failure is rarely the damage. It is that you spent two time and a crossed-off location to get the mediocre outcome, and neither of those is coming back.
Remember this

Budget for failure. Deciding to attempt a nine with a seven is a legitimate play when the downside is a single wound.

8 The campfire

Camping is the only way to recover, and the number of camps is your campaign clock.

Camping costs no time at all. It refills stamina to full, restores health, clears a status effect, and refreshes what you have available. It is the single most restorative thing you can do, and it is the thing that ends your campaign.

Because camping does not cost time. It costs a camp icon crossed off your journey log, and there is a finite printed row of them. Every campfire is a permanent tick towards the end of the story. That row, not the map, is the game's timer.

So the actual shape of Distant Skies is: push the crew until they are empty, wring the last useful action out of them, and only then pay a camp. Every camp taken while people still had stamina is a slice of campaign thrown away.

Remember this

Camps are the hourglass. Never camp with stamina still on the boards.

9 Events

The world takes a turn before you do.

Every player's turn opens with an event card read aloud: weather, wildlife, strangers, a piece of luck, a demand for a challenge. Some are pure gift, some are a bill, and a good many hand you a choice with a cost attached.

Events are the reason plans decay. You cannot line up a perfect five-tick turn the night before, because the first thing that happens is the world reminding you it was here first. Treat your plan as a preference and the event as an amendment.

Remember this

The event happens before your time, not during it. Read it, then decide the turn.

10 The plane

Your fourth character has its own health bar and it bleeds nights.

The plane is a card with a health track and a fuel requirement. Flying is fast, and it is the only way to cross the world at any speed, but it takes damage from what you fly through, and refuelling costs real resources at specific places.

Here is the vicious bit. When the plane takes damage at zero health, you do not lose the plane. You cross off a camp icon. Neglected maintenance is paid for directly in nights of your campaign, which makes the one-time Repair action the best-value tick on the whole track when the plane is looking rough.

The trap

Flying everywhere because walking feels slow. Fly costs two time, fuel, and hull. Travel costs one and asks for nothing. Long hops are for crossing regions, not for hopping between two places on the same page.

Remember this

An unrepaired plane converts every future knock straight into lost campaign. Repair is cheap; the alternative is not.

11 Quests and keywords

The book's memory, kept in your hands.

The storybook cannot remember what you did, so you remember it for it. Two components do that job.

Quest cards come out of passages and sit in front of you: go here, bring this, find them. They tell you what to do and where, and completing one usually means opening a specific numbered location with a specific thing in hand.

Keywords are the subtler one. Certain passages hand you a keyword, and later passages, sometimes an ocean away, are gated behind it: this option is only available if you have that keyword. Without it you read a shorter, worse paragraph and never learn what you missed.

This is why aimless exploring hurts twice. It burns the clock, and it also means the keyword you needed for the good branch of the totem passage is sitting unclaimed in a village three pages away.

Remember this

Open quests are a to-do list with map references on it. Play the list, not the map.

12 Combat

A separate little card game, and it starts when the story says so.

Fights are not resolved with skill checks. They pull out a different deck, a combat deck you build up over the campaign, and play out over rounds on a small board of enemies.

Each round the crew plays a handful of cards, each assigned to a character and aimed at an enemy. Damage lands on that enemy's grid, and where it lands matters: a grid fills according to adjacency rules, and an enemy is only defeated when every heart on its grid is covered. Other cards strip power tokens, or gain you block instead of dealing damage.

Then the enemies hit back immediately, for their base attack plus whatever power tokens they still hold. That is the tension: a card spent removing power is a card not spent covering hearts, and the fight goes long, which means it hurts more.

The trap

Spreading damage across every enemy so nothing dies. Damage is only worth anything when a grid is completely covered, and a wounded enemy hits exactly as hard as a fresh one. Kill things one at a time.

Remember this

Fights are not scored on damage dealt; they are scored on enemies removed. Everything else is delay.

13 Totems and the ending

You do not finish the world. You finish the errand and leave.

Scattered through the atlas are dormant totems. Restoring one takes the right quest resources delivered to the right place, and each one you wake marks an achievement box and brings the way home closer. There are many more of them out there than a single campaign needs, so which ones you go for is genuinely your plan, not the game's.

When enough are awake, the storybook's ending sections open up and the campaign concludes with a final confrontation. Reaching and completing an ending is worth a large chunk of your final score, and around it you count up the rest:

SourceWorth
Reaching an ending10
Adventure cards2 each, and totems 5
Quest cards completed1 each
Equipped ability cards1 each
Common resources1 per four
Difficulty+10 normal, +20 hard

Achievements also carry forward: they unlock cards you start a future campaign with, so the world you leave behind is a slightly kinder one for the next crew. Losing is rare and blunt, and mostly means the whole crew hit zero health.

Remember this

Totems are the win condition and the biggest scoring cards in the game. Everything else on the scoresheet is a rounding error you picked up on the way.

14 Your first campaign

How not to lose it: five habits to pick up immediately.

1. Pick a totem and go

Within your first session or two, name a target out loud and fly at it. Wandering is what the game wants you to do and what the clock punishes you for. You can always look at the pretty ruin next campaign; that is what the atlas is for.

2. Never camp early

Before you cross that icon off, look at every character board. Stamina left? Health nearly full? Then you are throwing away a night of the journey. Push one more turn.

3. Equip in the first hour

New crews spend their whole hand one point at a time on checks and end the campaign with bare character boards. Two or three good pieces of equipment early are worth more than a dozen +1s, because every check afterwards starts higher.

4. Repair before it costs a night

A single tick of time spent patching the plane is nothing. A knock on a plane at zero health costs a camp icon, and a camp icon is a chunk of your ending.

5. Let checks fail

You will be tempted to make every number. Do not. Look at the failure text, and if it is one wound and a shrug, take the shrug and keep your cards. The hand you saved is what gets you through the check that actually matters.

Remember this

Aim at a totem, camp late, equip early, and treat your hand of cards as a war chest for the checks you cannot afford to miss.
